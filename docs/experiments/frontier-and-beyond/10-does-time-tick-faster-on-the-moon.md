## Overview

Gravity Experiment 9 showed that a GPS clock, 20,000 kilometres above the Earth, gains about 38 millionths of a second a day on a clock on the ground, because two relativistic effects compete: gravity is weaker up there, which speeds a clock up, and the satellite is moving, which slows it down. Experiment 9 of this group showed that a clock higher by a metre ticks faster by about one part in 10¹⁶. This experiment applies the same two effects to a **new place: the Moon.**

In April 2026 four astronauts flew around the Moon on Artemis II, the first crewed lunar flight since 1972. Space agencies plan to put navigation and communication systems around the Moon, and such systems need clocks. In 2024 two physicists at NIST, Neil Ashby and Bijunath Patla, worked out what relativity says about a clock on the Moon: it should tick **about 56 millionths of a second a day faster** than a clock on the Earth (computed here: 55.97). In October 2026 the General Conference on Weights and Measures asked for an international lunar reference time scale. Nobody has yet compared a clock on the Moon with a clock on the Earth, so the 56 millionths is a calculation from relativity, with the same physics that has been tested with clocks on Earth, in orbit and across small heights, and not yet a measurement on the Moon.

This experiment lets the learner rebuild three ideas:

1. **A clock's rate is lowered by two things: sitting deep in gravity, and moving.** The fraction by which a clock ticks slower than one far from everything is the sum of `potential ÷ c²` (gravity) and `speed² ÷ (2 × c²)` (motion), where c is the speed of light. For a clock on the Earth's equator that is 6.97 × 10⁻¹⁰ (computed): almost all of it is gravity, and a little is the Earth's spin.
2. **The Moon's clock is lowered much less, so it gains.** On the Moon's surface three terms add up to only 4.87 × 10⁻¹¹ (computed): the Moon's own gravity (3.14 × 10⁻¹¹), the Earth's pull at the Moon's distance (1.15 × 10⁻¹¹) and the Moon's speed around the Earth (5.8 × 10⁻¹²). The difference, 6.48 × 10⁻¹⁰, is 55.97 microseconds a day (computed), which agrees with NIST's 56.02 to within 0.1 percent.
3. **Where a clock sits decides its rate, and the answer is not always "faster".** A clock in low orbit around the Earth runs slower than one on the ground (about 25 microseconds a day for the International Space Station, computed), a GPS clock faster (38.6, computed), a lunar-surface clock faster (56.0), and a clock in orbit around the Moon is a little slower than one on the Moon's surface below about 870 kilometres and faster above it (computed). The learner chooses the place and sees the terms.

It says honestly what it does **not** do: it does **not** model the real, slightly changing rate over a month (the Moon's orbit is not a circle), it does **not** model the Sun's gravity or the Moon's shape and internal mass, it does **not** define lunar time or compare time scales, and it does **not** say that a clock on the Moon has been measured.

The new ideas are (1) **adding the gravity and the motion effects to find a clock's rate** (the GPS experiment's two effects, used for two bodies at once), (2) **the Earth's pull at the Moon's distance as a third term**, (3) **a lunar time scale**, and (4) **a reference clock "far from everything"**. "A higher clock ticks faster" and "gravity slows clocks" are reused from Gravity Experiments 2 and 9, and "moving clocks run slow" from Relativity of Time and Motion Experiments 3 and 4.

## Learning Objective

After this experiment, the learner should understand:

1. **A clock's rate depends on gravity and motion together.** Compared with a clock far from everything, a clock ticks slower by `Φ ÷ c² + v² ÷ (2c²)`, where Φ is the gravitational potential (how deep in gravity the clock sits) and v its speed. Both terms lower the rate.
2. **The Earth's equator clock is lowered by 6.97 × 10⁻¹⁰** (gravity 6.953 × 10⁻¹⁰ and the Earth's spin 1.2 × 10⁻¹²), **and the Moon-surface clock by only 4.87 × 10⁻¹¹** (computed).
3. **So the Moon's clock gains 6.48 × 10⁻¹⁰, or 55.97 microseconds a day**, about 1.7 milliseconds a month and 20 milliseconds a year (computed). NIST's published value is 56.02 microseconds a day.
4. **The three terms of the Moon's clock:** the Moon's own gravity is the largest (3.14 × 10⁻¹¹), the Earth's pull at that distance is about a third as large (1.15 × 10⁻¹¹), and the speed term is the smallest (5.8 × 10⁻¹²).
5. **Place matters.** The same rule gives −24.6 microseconds a day for a clock on the International Space Station (slower), +38.6 for a GPS clock (faster, matching Gravity Experiment 9's 38) and +56.0 for the Moon's surface (all compared with the Earth's equator, computed).
6. **A clock orbiting the Moon** is slower than one on the Moon's surface below 1.5 Moon radii from its centre (an altitude of about 869 km) and faster above it: the same crossover as the GPS experiment's 1.5 Earth radii (computed).
7. **What is calculation and what is measurement.** The 56 microseconds is a calculation from relativity, as of October 2026; no clock on the Moon has yet been compared with one on the Earth, and ACES, a clock on the International Space Station built to test gravity's effect on clocks, had not published a result in the sources read.

## Physical Situation

### Reused
- **A higher clock ticks faster** (Gravity Experiments 2 and 9, and Experiment 9 of this group).
- **Moving clocks run slow** (Relativity of Time and Motion Experiments 3 and 4), here as `v² ÷ (2c²)` for small speeds.
- **The GPS crossover at 1.5 radii** (Gravity Experiment 9), applied to the Moon.
- **Circular orbits:** the speed of a circular orbit is `√(GM ÷ r)` (Gravity Experiment 6).

### What is new
- **Two bodies at once:** a clock on the Moon sits in the Moon's gravity and in the Earth's, and moves with the Moon around the Earth.
- **"Far from everything" as the reference** for adding the terms (the Earth equator's clock is then compared with it).
- **A lunar time scale** (named, not modelled).

### Simplifying assumptions (all stated to the learner in plain language)
- **Weak-field formula, one step.** A clock's rate is lowered by `Φ ÷ c² + v² ÷ (2c²)`; the terms are added as small fractions (the formula of Gravity Experiments 2 and 3 and 9).
- **Mean values.** The Moon is at its mean distance (384,400 km) and mean orbital speed (1.022 km/s); the real orbit is slightly elliptical and the real rate varies a little during a month, which the NIST study includes and this experiment does not.
- **Spherical bodies.** The Earth and the Moon are spheres with their equatorial or mean radius; the Earth's bulge and the Moon's uneven mass are ignored. (For the Earth's clock the equator is used, as in NIST's comparison.)
- **The Sun is left out.** Its pull is nearly the same on the Earth and the Moon, so it nearly cancels in the comparison.
- **Orbit clocks:** a clock in a circular orbit around a body has the orbital speed `√(GM ÷ r)`; for a clock orbiting the Moon the Moon's own motion around the Earth adds a squared speed (the cross term averages to zero over an orbit).
- **Published numbers are quoted as the sources state them.**

### Not introduced
Coordinate time and the time scales TT, TCG, TCL and UTC (named only), the exact weak-field metric, the Moon's orbit's variations, Lagrange points, how lunar navigation works, and how clocks would be flown or compared.

### The rules

- **A clock's lowering, relative to one far from everything:** `Φ ÷ c² + v² ÷ (2 × c²)`, where `Φ` is the sum of `GM ÷ distance` for each body that matters, and `v` is the clock's speed.
- **Earth's equator:** `GM_E ÷ (R_E c²) + (ω R_E)² ÷ (2 c²)`.
- **Moon's surface:** `GM_M ÷ (R_M c²) + GM_E ÷ (d c²) + v_M² ÷ (2c²)` (the Moon's spin is negligible but kept as a term).
- **A circular orbit around a body of mass M at radius r:** gravity `GM ÷ (r c²)` and speed `GM ÷ (2 r c²)`, so `1.5 × GM ÷ (r c²)`; around the Moon add the Earth's term and the Moon's own orbital-speed term.
- **The gain of a clock over the Earth's equator:** `(Earth's lowering) − (the clock's lowering)`; in microseconds a day, multiply by 86,400 × 10⁶.

## Prediction Activity

Two questions, both answered before Run is enabled. Not scored while the learner is predicting; the tutor marks each as matched or not matched in its explanation (the group's pattern). Each ends "You are not graded, and a guess is fine."

> **Prediction 1: faster or slower?** Two identical, perfect clocks are set to the same time. One is kept on the Earth's surface and the other is taken to the Moon's surface, where gravity is about a sixth of the Earth's, and left there. Compared with the clock on the Earth, will the clock on the Moon tick **slower**, at **the same rate**, or **faster**? Buttons: **Slower**, **The same**, **Faster**.
>
> **Prediction 2: by how much?** If the clock on the Moon ticks at a different rate, how far apart would the two clocks be after **one day**? Buttons: **About 56 nanoseconds (billionths of a second)**, **About 56 microseconds (millionths of a second)**, **About 56 milliseconds (thousandths of a second)**.

Right answers: Prediction 1 is **faster**; the natural wrong answers are "the same" (the Moon is quiet and small) and "slower" (the Moon moves; the clock is far from home). Prediction 2 is **about 56 microseconds** (55.97 µs, `timeScale(...)`); the natural wrong answers are "nanoseconds" (the effect sounds negligible) and "milliseconds" (a day is long). Prediction 2 gives away the 56 in its buttons; the reasons are for the tutor. The buttons give the number 56 (decision 4, approved on its default), which makes Prediction 2 a matter of choosing the unit; the alternative, "about 5.6, 56 or 560 microseconds", was not chosen.

The prediction texts do not give the sizes of the terms or the sign of the result.
