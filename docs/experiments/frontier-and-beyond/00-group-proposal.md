## Overview

The project's six groups so far teach settled physics: how time, space, gravity, black holes and the universe behave according to relativity and the measurements behind it. This group is different in one way and the same in every other. Each experiment is still a predict, experiment, observe, explain lesson on a tested physics model. What changes is the anchor: **each experiment starts from a result published or reported in 2022 to 2026** (a measurement, a detection or a mission), uses the project's existing physics to let the learner rebuild the idea behind it, and says plainly **how settled that result is.**

Because the owner intends this to be the last group, it is written to leave the learner **up to date across the whole subject**: the expanding universe and what fills it, tests of gravity and of time, black holes and gravitational waves, stars and spacecraft. A learner who finishes it should be able to read a news story on any of these and know what is solid, what is a hint and what is still unknown.

Two commitments make this safe to write, because "the latest findings" can be wrong or out of date by the time they are read:

1. **A status label on every experiment**, in plain words, chosen from four: *Settled* (many independent confirmations), *Strong evidence* (one clear result, widely accepted), *Contested* (real data, but experts disagree about what it means) and *Open* (we do not yet know). The label and the date it was written ("as of October 2026") are shown to the learner.
2. **A "facts that may change" table in every specification**, listing every dated fact, its source and the date it was checked, so the owner can re-check them later in one place. Since there will be no later group to correct them, this table is how the group stays honest after it is finished.

The group would not claim to be a news feed. It teaches the ideas that let a learner read the next headline: what a "sigma" is, what it takes to call a measurement a discovery, why a clean detection can test an old theorem, why a tension can vanish, and why a surprising object does not yet mean new physics.

### Part A: the expanding universe and what fills it

**1. Two measurements of the expansion rate: why don't they agree? *(the Hubble tension)***
- **Anchor.** Measurements of the Hubble constant from the early universe (the cosmic microwave background, about 67 km/s/Mpc) and from the nearby universe (the distance ladder with Cepheid stars, about 73) disagree by about 5 sigma. In 2024 and 2025, new James Webb Space Telescope observations of the same Cepheids matched the Hubble Space Telescope's, which rules out one proposed explanation (crowding); another team's measurement with a different method (red-giant stars) gives about 70, between the two. **Contested** (the disagreement is real; its cause is not known).
- **Objective.** What "sigma" means (the gap between two measurements divided by their combined uncertainty), why 5 sigma matters, and what the gap would mean for the age of the universe.
- **Model.** The tension in sigma, and the Hubble time (1 ÷ H₀) from Cosmology Experiments 1, 2 and 6 (about 14.5 billion years for 67.4 and 13.4 for 73.0). No new physics.
- **Prediction.** Would sharper pictures of the Cepheids close the gap? (So far, no.) Then the learner sets how much larger the uncertainties would have to be for the disagreement to vanish.
- **Builds on.** Cosmology 1, 2 and 6.

**2. Does dark energy change? *(DESI)***
- **Anchor.** The DESI survey's 2025 data release, combined with supernova and microwave-background data, prefers dark energy that weakens over time, at about 3 to 4 sigma depending on the combination of datasets, while other analyses find a weaker preference (one finds about 1 to 2 sigma from late-time data alone); DESI's July 2026 hydrogen-gas results lean back toward a constant dark energy. **Contested.**
- **Objective.** What an evolving dark energy means (its strength changes with cosmic time), how small the effect on measured distances is, and how a hint, a 3 sigma and a discovery differ.
- **Model.** The standard two-number description w(a) = w₀ + w_a(1 − a), and the expansion rate and distances it gives, compared with a constant dark energy (Cosmology 5, 6 and 10). Textbook formula, to be checked.
- **Prediction.** For an evolving dark energy like the one DESI hints at, how different would the distance to a galaxy look from the constant case: a few percent, or a few tenths of a percent, compared with typical measurement errors?
- **Builds on.** Cosmology 5, 6, 10 and Experiment 1.

**3. How lumpy is the universe? A tension that went away *(KiDS-Legacy)***
- **Anchor.** For years, surveys that measure how galaxies' light is bent by matter (weak lensing) found a universe slightly smoother than the microwave background predicts, the "S8 tension". The final KiDS-Legacy analysis (2025) found S8 = 0.815, in agreement with the microwave background to 0.73 sigma, after better calibration. **Strong evidence** that this tension was a measurement problem, though other surveys continue.
- **Objective.** A tension can disappear when a measurement is improved, which is why Experiment 1's tension is not yet a discovery. Also what S8 measures (how clumpy matter is, from the sizes of clumps and the amount of matter).
- **Model.** The definition S8 = σ₈ √(Ω_m ÷ 0.3), sigma, and the growth of clumps from Cosmology Experiment 9. Textbook definition, to be checked.
- **Prediction.** If a survey's calibration error were corrected, would a 2.3 sigma tension shrink, stay or grow? (Then the learner sees the actual 2025 shift.)
- **Builds on.** Cosmology 7 and 9, and Experiment 1.

**4. Can the universe weigh a neutrino? *(DESI's limit on neutrino mass)* ◇**
- **Anchor.** Laboratory experiments that watch neutrinos change type say the three masses together must add up to at least about 0.06 electron-volts (about 0.10 if the order of the masses is the other way round). Combining DESI's 2025 map with the microwave background gives an upper limit of about 0.064 electron-volts, leaving almost no room, and in some dataset combinations the cosmological and laboratory results are in tension at 2.5 to 5 sigma. **Contested.**
- **Objective.** A particle with a tiny mass acts like light when it is hot and like matter when it is cooler. The universe's expansion cools it, so the more a neutrino weighs, the earlier it starts acting like matter and the more it changes how galaxies grow (uses Spacetime Geometry Experiment 5 and Experiment 1's sigma).
- **Model.** The cosmic neutrino temperature (about 1.95 K today, from the microwave background's 2.725 K), the redshift at which a neutrino of a given mass stops being "relativistic" (for 0.06 eV, about 110), the neutrinos' share of the universe's mass-energy, and the textbook rough estimate that small-scale clustering is weakened by about eight times that share (to be checked).
- **Prediction.** If the three masses add up to 0.06 electron-volts, do the neutrinos start acting like matter before or after the microwave background was released (a redshift of about 1,090)?
- **Builds on.** Cosmology 3 and 9, and Spacetime Geometry 5. The most complex of Part A.

**5. How early can galaxies form? *(JWST's earliest galaxies)***
- **Anchor.** In 2025 JWST confirmed MoM-z14, a galaxy seen as it was about 280 million years after the Big Bang (redshift 14.44), a new record, beating the 2024 record of 14.18. JWST has found far more bright galaxies this early than many models made before it expected. **Strong evidence** that galaxies form this early; **Open** why there are so many.
- **Objective.** What a redshift of 14.4 means in time (how long after the Big Bang) and distance, and why finding bright galaxies so early is a puzzle for galaxy formation but not a problem for the Big Bang itself.
- **Model.** Age and distance at a given redshift from Cosmology Experiments 6 and 8, and the growth of structure from Experiment 9. No new physics.
- **Prediction.** At a redshift of 14.4, is the universe closer to 1 percent, 2 percent or 10 percent of its present age? (About 2 percent.)
- **Builds on.** Cosmology 6, 8 and 9.

**6. Where is the dark matter? The null results *(LZ, 2025)***
- **Anchor.** The LZ experiment (a tank of liquid xenon a mile underground) reported in December 2025 the world's strongest limits on light dark-matter particles, from 417 days of data: no sign of particles of 3 to 9 times a proton's mass, and a first strong detection (4.5 sigma) of neutrinos from the Sun's core hitting xenon nuclei, which means the experiment is nearing the "neutrino fog" where solar neutrinos mimic dark matter. **Strong evidence** about what dark matter is not; **Open** what it is.
- **Objective.** How a search with no detections still teaches something (each non-detection rules out particles that would have produced a signal), why experiments need huge exposure, and why a floor of unavoidable neutrino signals matters.
- **Model.** Expected number of events = rate × mass × time, with the learner choosing the exposure and the interaction strength, and the 4.5 sigma of the solar-neutrino signal (the sigma idea of Experiment 1). Simple counting, no new physics.
- **Prediction.** If a particle's interaction were ten times weaker than the current limit, would the detector, run ten times longer, expect to see it?
- **Builds on.** Cosmology 4 and 7, and Experiment 1.

**7. Do we need dark matter, or does gravity change? Wide binary stars *(Gaia)* ◇**
- **Anchor.** Pairs of stars very far apart orbit so slowly that gravity is very weak. If gravity changed at such tiny accelerations (the idea called MOND), their speeds would be about 40 percent higher than Newton's law says. Two teams analysing the same Gaia data disagree: one reports Newton's law preferred at 19 sigma, another reports a boost at 10 sigma. **Contested.**
- **Objective.** What a "modified gravity" would look like in a testable number, why wide binaries are a clean test (dark matter does not matter for two stars), and how two careful analyses of one data set can still disagree.
- **Model.** The Newtonian orbital speed v = √(GM÷r) against separation r, and the MOND relation v⁴ = GM·a₀ in the weak limit, with the learner choosing the separation. Builds on Cosmology 4's galaxy rotation curves. Textbook formulas, to be checked.
- **Prediction.** For two stars 10,000 times the Earth-Sun distance apart, would a MOND-like gravity make their orbital speed noticeably higher than Newton's? By how much?
- **Builds on.** Cosmology 4, and Gravity 6 (orbits).

### Part B: testing gravity and time

**8. Does antimatter fall down? *(ALPHA-g)***
- **Anchor.** In 2023 the ALPHA collaboration at CERN released trapped antihydrogen atoms and watched where they went. They fell, as ordinary matter does: the ratio of antimatter's gravitational acceleration to ordinary matter's was 0.75 with an uncertainty of about 0.2, compatible with 1 (a 2023 Nature paper). Later campaigns with laser cooling aim at much higher precision, and their results are not yet confirmed here. **Strong evidence** (antimatter falls down, to within about 20 percent).
- **Objective.** Why physicists ask this (does every kind of matter fall alike: the equivalence principle of Gravity Experiment 1?), what a measurement of 0.75 ± 0.2 can and cannot tell us, and how to read a ratio with an error bar.
- **Model.** Free fall with gravitational acceleration ratio r: the distance an atom falls in a given time, and how far a measured r is from 1 and from −1 (a "falls up" case) in sigma. Newtonian free fall plus sigma; no new physics.
- **Prediction.** If antimatter fell up (a ratio of −1), would the measured 0.75 ± 0.2 be compatible with it?
- **Builds on.** Gravity 1 and Experiment 1.

**9. Can a clock tell how high it is? *(optical lattice clocks)* ◇**
- **Anchor.** In 2022 a JILA clock made of ultracold strontium atoms was precise enough (7.6 parts in 10²¹) to see the gravitational redshift across a single millimetre. Clocks this good could redefine the second (a proposal that could be presented as early as 2026, with adoption no earlier than 2030) and measure heights on Earth by the rate they tick. **Settled** that gravity changes a clock's rate at this scale (the 2022 measurement); the redefinition is a plan, not a result.
- **Objective.** A clock higher up ticks faster, by a tiny amount that grows with height, and today's best clocks can tell the difference over a millimetre.
- **Model.** The weak-field rate difference Δf ÷ f = gh ÷ c² (Gravity Experiments 2 and 9): about 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶ per metre, so about 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁹ for a millimetre.
- **Prediction.** Two identical clocks, one 1 metre above the other: after a year, how far apart would they be, in microseconds, nanoseconds or picoseconds? (About 3.4 nanoseconds.)
- **Builds on.** Gravity 2 and 9.

**10. Does time tick faster on the Moon? *(lunar clocks, Artemis II and ACES)***
- **Anchor.** Artemis II, with four astronauts, flew around the Moon on April 6 and 7, 2026, the first crewed lunar flight since 1972. As space agencies plan lunar navigation, a 2024 NIST study calculated that a clock on the Moon gains about 56 microseconds per day compared with one on Earth. The European ACES clock mission on the International Space Station (launched April 2025) aims to measure gravitational redshift directly (to about 3 parts in a million) but was still being commissioned when last reported, so it has no result yet. **Strong evidence** (a calculation from general relativity, not yet a measurement by a clock on the Moon).
- **Objective.** The two competing effects on a clock's rate, slower for speed and faster for weaker gravity, and why the Moon's net result is a gain. This is the GPS experiment applied to a new place.
- **Model.** The weak-field clock-rate formula of Gravity Experiment 9: gravitational term GM÷(rc²) and speed term v²÷(2c²). A quick check by hand reproduces the NIST figure: Earth's surface clock lags by about 6.97 × 10⁻¹⁰, the Moon-surface clock by about 4.9 × 10⁻¹¹, so the Moon gains about 6.5 × 10⁻¹⁰, or about 56 microseconds per day (to be checked against the paper).
- **Prediction.** Will a clock on the Moon's surface tick slower than, the same as or faster than one on Earth's surface?
- **Builds on.** Gravity 2 and 9, and Relativity of Time and Motion 3.

**11. The fastest spacecraft: how much does its clock lag? *(Parker Solar Probe)* ◇**
- **Anchor.** On December 24, 2024 NASA's Parker Solar Probe passed the Sun at 6.17 million kilometres from its surface at 192.22 kilometres per second, the fastest human-made object. **Settled** (the distance and speed are NASA's; the clock lag is a calculation).
- **Objective.** Near the Sun, speed and gravity both slow a clock, unlike at the Moon where they compete. At 192 km/s the speed term alone is about 2 × 10⁻⁷, so the probe's clock loses about 18 milliseconds a day to its speed and a similar amount to the Sun's gravity.
- **Model.** The same weak-field formula as Experiment 10, applied at the probe's distance from the Sun's centre. A check by hand gives a rate about 4.2 × 10⁻⁷ below a distant clock (to be checked).
- **Prediction.** At the Sun's closest approach, do speed and gravity both slow the probe's clock, both speed it up, or work in opposite directions?
- **Builds on.** Experiment 10, Gravity 9, and Relativity of Time and Motion 3.

**12. The star that may be about to explode: what is Betelgeuse, and does it have a companion? *(Gemini North, 2025)***
- **Anchor.** Betelgeuse, a red supergiant in Orion, dimmed sharply in 2019 to 2020 and has a slow brightness cycle of about six years. In July 2025 a NASA-led team using the Gemini North telescope reported a faint companion, named Siwarha, of about 1.6 solar masses on a 5.94-year orbit. The detection is only about **1.5 sigma** (a hint, not a discovery), though it sits where it was predicted to be. **Settled** (Betelgeuse is a red supergiant, near the end of its life); **Open** (the companion). The Sun will become a red giant too, in about five billion years.
- **Objective.** What a red giant or supergiant is (a star that has used up the hydrogen in its core and swollen), how big one is next to the Solar System, and how to read a 1.5 sigma hint (reusing Experiment 1's sigma).
- **Model.** Radius from luminosity and temperature (L = 4πR²σT⁴), Kepler's third law for the orbit, and sigma. A rough check puts Betelgeuse's radius at about 3.5 Earth-Sun distances, past Mars and short of Jupiter (to be checked).
- **Prediction.** If Betelgeuse took the Sun's place, would it swallow Earth, Mars, or Jupiter?
- **Check at specification time.** The summaries give the companion's separation as about 4 astronomical units, but Kepler's third law with a supergiant's mass gives a larger orbit; the two must be reconciled in the paper (the paper may be giving a different quantity). A January 2026 paper (arXiv 2601.00470) came up and was not read. Whether the companion has been confirmed since July 2025 must be checked.
- **Builds on.** Black Holes 9 (the end of a star's life) and this group's Experiment 1 (sigma).

**13. Catching a supernova in the act *(SN 2023ixf)***
- **Anchor.** SN 2023ixf exploded in May 2023 in the galaxy M101, one of the nearest supernovae in decades. Telescopes had photographed the star before it blew up, so the progenitor is known to be a luminous, dusty red supergiant (Hubble and Spitzer archives), and spectra taken within hours of the explosion saw gas the star had shed in its last years. **Strong evidence** that a red supergiant exploded; **Contested** how massive it was (teams' estimates differ; to be checked).
- **Objective.** A supernova releases about as much energy as the Sun will in its whole life, but almost all of it leaves as neutrinos, not light; what the visible flash is; and why seeing the star before it exploded is rare and valuable.
- **Model.** Energy of a core collapse from the neutron star's binding energy (about GM²÷R, near 10⁴⁶ joules; a textbook estimate, to be checked), the small share that becomes visible light, and light travel time to M101 (about 21 million light-years). Reuses Spacetime Geometry 5 (energy) and Black Holes 9.
- **Prediction.** Of the energy released in the collapse, is the visible light about half, about a tenth, or a tiny fraction?
- **Builds on.** Experiment 12, Black Holes 9, and Spacetime Geometry 5.

**14. What makes a white dwarf explode? *(the double-detonation remnant SNR 0509-67.5)* ◇**
- **Anchor.** A white dwarf in a close pair can explode as a Type Ia supernova; these are the "standard candles" behind Experiments 1 and 2 of this group. In July 2025 a team using the Very Large Telescope's MUSE instrument found two separate shells of calcium in the 300-year-old remnant SNR 0509-67.5, the fingerprint of a "double detonation" in which a helium layer ignites first and sets off the core. This points to a white dwarf exploding **below** the 1.4-solar-mass Chandrasekhar limit. **Strong evidence** for this one remnant; **Open** how common the route is.
- **Objective.** Why a white dwarf has a maximum mass (Black Holes 9), the two ways it could be pushed to explode, and why that matters for measuring the universe: if Type Ia explosions are not all alike, distances built on them need care.
- **Model.** The white dwarf mass-radius relation and Chandrasekhar limit from Black Holes 9, and the idea that brightness depends on the mass of nickel made. Textbook; to be checked.
- **Prediction.** Must a white dwarf reach 1.4 solar masses before it can explode?
- **Builds on.** Black Holes 9, and this group's Experiments 1 and 2.

**15. How big is a neutron star, and where do they end? *(NICER and GW230529)***
- **Anchor.** NICER, an X-ray telescope on the International Space Station, measured the pulsar PSR J0437−4715 (1.42 times the Sun's mass) to be about 11 to 15 kilometres across in radius, with an earlier analysis giving 11.4 km, which constrains what matter is like inside. In 2024 the gravitational-wave event GW230529 showed a neutron star merging with an object of about 3.6 solar masses, in the gap between the heaviest neutron stars and the lightest known black holes. **Strong evidence** (the size); **Open** (whether the 3.6-solar-mass object is a black hole).
- **Objective.** A neutron star is far smaller than a city yet heavier than the Sun, how close it is to being a black hole (its compactness, GM ÷ Rc²), and why a heavier object eventually cannot hold itself up.
- **Model.** Compactness and surface gravity from mass and radius, and the black-hole size 2GM÷c² of Black Holes Experiment 1: for 1.4 solar masses the black-hole radius is about 4.1 km, so a neutron star of 11.4 km is about 2.8 times larger than that. Reuses Black Holes 1 and 9.
- **Prediction.** If a neutron star of 1.4 solar masses were squeezed to a radius of 6 km, would it become a black hole? (No: the black-hole radius is 4.1 km.)
- **Builds on.** Black Holes 1 and 9, and Gravity 2.

**16. Where does gold come from? *(the magnetar flare of 2004)* ◇**
- **Anchor.** In April 2025 a team reported that the delayed gamma rays after the giant flare of the magnetar SGR 1806-20 (December 2004) match the glow of freshly made heavy elements, "direct evidence" that a magnetar flare can forge gold, platinum and uranium. They estimate one such flare made a mass of heavy elements of the order of a third of the Earth's mass, and that such flares could supply a few to ten percent of the heavy elements in the Milky Way (the figures differ between summaries; to be checked). **Contested**: it is one reanalysis of old data, and neutron star mergers remain the best-established source.
- **Objective.** Heavy elements are made when neutrons are added to atoms faster than they decay (the "r-process"); where that happens (a neutron star merger, a supernova, a magnetar flare); and how to compare sources by amount made times how often they happen.
- **Model.** Simple counting: mass per event × events per year × time against the Galaxy's total. Reuses Gravitational Waves 3 and 4 (neutron star mergers) and Experiment 15.
- **Prediction.** Could the flares of one kind of object, seen about once in a few hundred years in the Galaxy, supply a noticeable share of its gold?
- **Builds on.** Gravitational Waves 3 and 4, and Spacetime Geometry 5.

**17. A star that vanished: can a star become a black hole without exploding? *(M31-2014-DS1)***
- **Anchor.** A yellow supergiant of about a dozen solar masses in the Andromeda galaxy brightened in infrared in 2014, then faded from 2017 until, by 2022, it was gone in visible light; the team's reading (Science, 12 February 2026; arXiv 2410.14778) is that its core collapsed to a black hole and the outer layers fell in, with no bright supernova (a "failed supernova"). An earlier candidate in NGC 6946 is similar. **Open**: other explanations (for example a merger of two stars) have not been excluded for such events.
- **Objective.** Why some massive stars may skip the explosion, what a surveyor would see (a star dimming and disappearing rather than a flash), and why this matters for how many black holes there are and for the "mass gap" of Experiment 20.
- **Model.** The energy that must be given to the outer layers to unbind them, against the energy the collapsing core can pass on (a textbook estimate, to be checked), and the fraction of massive stars that would have to fail to match the number of black holes (a published estimate, to be checked).
- **Prediction.** If a star's core collapses and the outer layers fall in, will a telescope see a bright flash?
- **Builds on.** Experiments 12 and 13, and Black Holes 1 and 9.

### Part D: black holes and gravitational waves

**18. Does a black hole's surface area ever shrink? *(GW250114 and Hawking's area theorem)***
- **Anchor.** In January 2025 the LIGO detectors recorded GW250114, the clearest gravitational-wave signal so far, from two black holes of about 33.6 and 32.2 times the Sun's mass. Its clarity let the team measure the horizon area before and after, and the total area rose, as Hawking's 1971 area theorem says it must (about 240,000 square kilometres before, 400,000 after, in the Northwestern press office's report; the paper was published in Physical Review Letters in September 2025). **Strong evidence**, from one very clear event.
- **Objective.** The area of a spinning black hole's horizon, why it cannot shrink even while the merger radiates energy as gravitational waves, and how that links to the "entropy" of Black Holes Experiments 8 and 10.
- **Model.** A = 8π(GM/c²)²(1 + √(1 − χ²)) from the project's spinning black hole module; the learner sets two black holes' masses and spins and the fraction of mass radiated. A rough check gives about 240,000 km² before and about 380,000 km² after for the published masses and a final spin near 0.7 (to be checked).
- **Prediction.** If about 5 percent of the mass leaves as gravitational waves, does the total horizon area go down, stay the same or go up?
- **Builds on.** Black Holes 8 and 10, and Gravitational Waves 1 to 4.

**19. Do black holes ring like bells? *(GW250114's ringdown)***
- **Anchor.** After two black holes merge, the new one "rings", giving off tones fixed only by its mass and spin. GW250114 was clear enough to detect at least two tones, and the second's mass and spin matched the first's, as Einstein's theory says for a black hole with no other features, to within tens of percent. **Strong evidence**, from one event, at modest precision.
- **Objective.** A black hole is described by just its mass and spin ("no hair"), so its ringing pitch and fade time must follow from those two numbers, and a second tone gives a second, independent test.
- **Model.** The standard fit for the main ringing frequency and decay time of a spinning black hole as a function of mass and spin (published fits, to be checked), with the learner choosing the mass and spin. For example, a remnant of about 63 solar masses and spin 0.7 rings at roughly 270 hertz.
- **Prediction.** If the remnant spun faster, would its ringing pitch be higher, lower or the same?
- **Builds on.** Black Holes 8, and Gravitational Waves 1, 2 and 4.

**20. Black holes that should not exist: the heaviest merger *(GW231123)***
- **Anchor.** In July 2025 the LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA collaboration announced GW231123 (detected in November 2023), the most massive merger seen so far: black holes of about 100 and 140 solar masses made one of about 225. Both lie in the "mass gap" of roughly 60 to 130 solar masses where stars are not expected to leave black holes, and both were spinning very fast. **Open** (explanations include earlier mergers, and a primordial origin has been proposed).
- **Objective.** Why single stars should not leave black holes in the gap (Black Holes Experiment 9), how two black holes below the gap can merge into one inside it, and why the spins give a clue (a simple merger's remnant spins slower than the reported spins).
- **Model.** Mass after a merger (the sum minus the energy radiated) and the spin of the remnant of two equal non-spinning black holes (about 0.69, to be checked).
- **Prediction.** Can two black holes that each formed from a single star, below the gap, merge into one inside the gap?
- **Builds on.** Black Holes 8 and 9, and Gravitational Waves 1 to 4.

**21. Can we photograph a black hole's shadow? *(Event Horizon Telescope)***
- **Anchor.** The Event Horizon Telescope imaged the black holes in the galaxy M87 and at the centre of the Milky Way: a bright ring about 42 millionths of an arcsecond across for M87\* (43.9 in later analyses) and about 51.8 for Sagittarius A\*. Their sizes match what general relativity predicts for their masses despite the black holes differing by a factor of about 1,500 in mass. The ring's brightness pattern in M87\* has changed from year to year (2017 to 2021). **Strong evidence.**
- **Objective.** The shadow is much bigger than the black hole (about 2.6 times its horizon), because gravity bends light into it (Black Holes Experiment 4), and the shadow's angular size follows from mass and distance alone.
- **Model.** Shadow diameter ≈ 2√27 GM÷c², divided by the distance, as a sky angle. A check by hand gives about 40 millionths of an arcsecond for M87\* and about 54 for Sagittarius A\* (to be checked).
- **Prediction.** M87\* has about 1,500 times the mass of Sagittarius A\* but is about 2,000 times farther: would its shadow look bigger, smaller or about the same in the sky?
- **Builds on.** Black Holes 1 and 4, and Gravity 8 (light bending).

**22. Does the universe hum? *(pulsar timing arrays)* ◇**
- **Anchor.** In 2023, teams timing the arrival of radio pulses from millisecond pulsars found evidence for a background of very slow gravitational waves, likely from pairs of supermassive black holes. A combined analysis (IPTA DR3) was expected to reach 5 sigma "in the coming months" as of the 2025 reports found; whether it has been published by now must be checked. **Strong evidence**; a firmer result expected.
- **Objective.** Gravitational waves have a huge range of frequencies, from audio (LIGO) down to one cycle in many years (pulsar timing), and what sets the frequency is the size of the orbit.
- **Model.** The wave frequency is twice the orbital frequency, and Kepler's law gives the orbital period for two supermassive black holes at a given separation. For example, two black holes of a billion solar masses each, 0.01 parsec apart, orbit in about 2 years, so the gravitational waves have a frequency of about 30 billionths of a hertz. (To be checked.)
- **Prediction.** For pairs of supermassive black holes, would the gravitational waves be at a higher or lower pitch than LIGO's black holes of a few tens of solar masses?
- **Builds on.** Gravitational Waves 1, 2 and 4, and Gravity 6.

**23. How fast can a black hole grow? The "little red dots" *(JWST)* ◇**
- **Anchor.** JWST has found many compact red sources in the young universe, and in 2026 reports argue that at least some are "black hole stars": a growing black hole wrapped in a thick cocoon of gas. Their black holes look surprisingly large for the early universe. **Open** (their nature is still debated); this is the least settled experiment in the group, and the specification would say so at the top.
- **Objective.** There is a speed limit on how fast a black hole can feed (light pushes back on the gas), so growth from a stellar seed takes a minimum time, and the early universe may not have had enough.
- **Model.** Growth at the Eddington limit, M(t) = M₀ exp(t ÷ t_growth), with a growth time of about 45 million years for a standard efficiency of 10 percent (textbook value, to be checked), and the age of the universe at a given redshift from Cosmology Experiment 6. For example, 10 solar masses to 100 million solar masses takes about 16 growth times, about 0.7 billion years.
- **Prediction.** Could a black hole of 10 solar masses, feeding at the limit, reach 100 million solar masses within the first 700 million years?
- **Builds on.** Cosmology 5 (of this group) and 6, and Black Holes 1 and 9.

### Part E: our neighbourhood

**24. A visitor from another star: how far does the Sun bend its path? *(3I/ATLAS)***
- **Anchor.** On July 1, 2025 the ATLAS survey found 3I/ATLAS, only the third object known to have come from another star. Its orbit is a hyperbola with an eccentricity of about 6.1, its closest approach to the Sun (October 2025) was about 1.36 times the Earth-Sun distance, and it arrived at about 58 kilometres per second relative to the Sun. **Settled** that it is interstellar. Claims about its age or composition are not part of the experiment, which uses only the orbit.
- **Objective.** Why some paths around the Sun are closed (ellipses) and others open (hyperbolas), what speed makes the difference (the escape speed), and why a very fast visitor's path bends only a little.
- **Model.** The orbit module of Gravity Experiment 6 extended to speeds above escape speed: closest-approach speed √(v∞² + 2GM/r), eccentricity e = 1 + r·v∞²÷GM, total bend 2·arcsin(1/e). For 3I/ATLAS these give about 68 km/s, an eccentricity of about 6.2 (matching the reported 6.1) and a bend of about 19 degrees (to be checked).
- **Prediction.** Would the Sun's gravity bend a path like this one by more than 90 degrees, by about 20 degrees, or hardly at all?
- **Builds on.** Gravity 6 (orbits), and Black Holes 3.
