## Overview

A **neutron star** is the core left behind when a massive star explodes (Experiment 13): up to about 2 Suns of matter packed into a ball about as wide as a city. Black Holes Experiment 9 taught what holds it up (neutrons pushing back) and that a white dwarf cannot be heavier than 1.4 Suns. Black Holes Experiment 1 used 12 km as a round value. This experiment asks where that number comes from, and how close such a star is to being a black hole.

In 2024 the NICER X-ray telescope on the International Space Station measured **PSR J0437−4715**, the nearest and brightest millisecond pulsar, and gave the tightest radius yet: 11.36 km, with a give-or-take of about 5 to 8 percent (NASA). NICER measures the size from X-ray pulses from hot spots on the surface; the star's gravity bends the light, which is how the radius can be read (NASA's science note). A new NICER analysis of another pulsar, PSR J0614−3329 (September 2026), gives 9.88 to 12.77 km (68 percent range), which overlaps the first. At the heavy end, a June 2026 preprint puts the maximum neutron star mass at 2.2 to 2.3 Suns under its assumptions, and in 2023 the gravitational-wave event GW230529 showed a neutron star merging with an object of about 3.6 Suns, in the range where it is not clear whether such an object is a neutron star or a black hole.

This experiment asks three questions:

1. **How big is a neutron star, and how close is it to being a black hole?** The learner sets a mass and a radius and sees the star next to its own black-hole size (2GM÷c², Black Holes 1) and the ratio of the two.
2. **How strong is gravity on its surface, and what does that do?** Surface gravity (GM÷R²) and how much slower a clock on the surface ticks (Gravity 2, Black Holes 2).
3. **Where do neutron stars end?** A mass scale showing the white dwarf limit, the range where neutrons hold the star up, the range that is unknown, and where GW230529's heavier object sits, with no verdict.

No new physics: the black-hole radius of Black Holes 1, the white dwarf size and limits of Black Holes 9, the weak-field clock rate of Gravity 2 (here at strong gravity, see decision 4), and plain GM÷R². What is new is the evidence and the question of where the ladder ends.

## Learning Objective

By the end the learner can say, in their own words:

1. A neutron star of about 1.4 Suns is about 11 to 12 km in radius (about 23 km across), measured by NICER to about 5 to 8 percent.
2. Its black-hole radius (2GM÷c²) would be about 4.2 km, so the star is about 2.7 times larger than a black hole of its mass: close, but not a black hole.
3. Compared with the Earth, the Sun and a white dwarf, a neutron star is by far the closest ordinary object to being a black hole (the ratio of radius to black-hole radius, on a log scale).
4. Gravity at its surface is about 1.5 × 10¹² m/s² (about 150 billion times Earth's), and a clock there ticks about 20 percent slower than a distant one.
5. There is a maximum mass: above it, nothing known holds the star up. Published estimates are about 2.2 to 2.3 Suns, but they depend on assumptions about dense matter, and the range from about 2 to 3 Suns is "not known" (Black Holes 9).
6. GW230529's 3.6-Sun object lies in a range where we do not know whether it is a neutron star or a black hole: **strong evidence for the measurement, open for its nature**.

## Physical Situation

A neutron star of mass M and radius R, not spinning (a stated simplification: real ones spin, and J0437 spins at about 174 times a second, which flattens it slightly; this is not in the model), seen from far away. NICER's measurement is of the star's equatorial radius; the model treats the star as a ball.

## Physics Model (`src/physics/neutronStarSize.ts`, proposed)

No new physics. All numbers in the module, tested. It imports `horizonRadiusKm` (Black Holes 1) and the limit constants and `whiteDwarfRadiusKm` (Black Holes 9), as Experiment 14 did, and nothing else.

| Part | What it computes | Check at specification time |
|---|---|---|
| **Black-hole radius and the ratio** | `r_s = 2GM/c²` = 2.953 km per Sun (Black Holes 1). For 1.418 Suns: 4.19 km; 11.36 ÷ 4.19 = **2.71**. For 1.4 Suns: 4.13 km; 12 ÷ 4.13 = 2.9. | The formula is Black Holes 1's. The 1.418 and 11.36 are the paper's |
| **Compactness** | `C = GM/(Rc²) = r_s ÷ (2R)`. For 1.418 Suns and 11.36 km: **0.184**. For the Sun: 2.1 × 10⁻⁶. For a 1 Sun white dwarf (5,660 km): 5.2 × 10⁻⁴. | Textbook definition; the numbers are the module's own |
| **Surface gravity** | `g = GM/R²`. For 1.418 Suns and 11.36 km: **about 1.5 × 10¹² m/s²**, about 1.5 × 10¹¹ times Earth's 9.81. | Newtonian value; real surface gravity needs a correction at this compactness (decision 4) |
| **Clock rate at the surface** | `sqrt(1 − r_s/R)` (the Schwarzschild factor of Black Holes 2): for the star above **0.795**, so a surface clock ticks about 20 percent slow compared with a distant one. | Black Holes 2's formula; valid outside a spherical mass |
| **Mean density** | `M ÷ (4/3 π R³)`: about 4.6 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³; a sugar-cube (1 cm³) of it, about 4.6 × 10¹¹ kg, against NASA's "about 1 trillion kilograms". | NASA's figure is a round number; the page says "about" |

The module records them as published data and does no calculation on them. | See "Conflicts between sources": the gap is "3 to 5 Suns" in the LIGO summary and "2 to 3 Suns" in Black Holes 9 |
| **How far an answer is from a published value** | `(value − published) ÷ give-or-take`, with the asymmetric give-or-take chosen by sign: 6 km is **5.4 km below** 11.36, about **8.5 times** the lower give-or-take of 0.63. A reading aid only; the intervals are not Gaussian. | Arithmetic from the paper's figures |

Functions (proposed): `blackHoleRadiusKm` (imported), `radiusRatio(massSolar, radiusKm)`, `compactness`, `surfaceGravity`, `gravityInEarths`, `surfaceClockRate`, `meanDensity`, `isInsideHorizon(massSolar, radiusKm)`, `ladderEntries()` (the published data), `distanceInGiveOrTakes`, `sizeAnswer()` and `gravityAnswer()` (the predictions), and `runNeutronStarSize(settings)` returning all numbers at once. A radius at or below `2GM/c²` is not an error in the module: it returns "inside its own black-hole radius" and the surface-based numbers (clock rate, compactness above 0.5) are reported as "no star: a black hole" instead. A non-positive mass or radius throws `RangeError`.

**What the model does not do:** it does not compute a radius from the physics of dense matter (the radius is an input, not a result), does not model spin, does not model the real shape, does not compute the maximum mass, does not decide what GW230529's object is, and does not simulate NICER's measurement. It does not use the "9/8" limit on the smallest stable star (decision 5).

## Prediction Activity

Two predictions, made before pressing Run. The page tells the learner (the owner's rule of 2026-10-23) that predictions are guesses and do not change the pictures or tables. Neither the introduction nor a button gives either answer (§12).

**Prediction 1: would it become a black hole?** *"A neutron star has about 1.4 times the Sun's mass and a radius of about 12 km. A black hole of 1.4 Suns has a radius of about 4.1 km. If this star were squeezed to a radius of 6 km, would it be a black hole?"* Buttons: **Yes, it would be a black hole**, **No, it would still be bigger than a black hole of its mass**. Right answer: **No** (6 km is 1.45 times the 4.13 km). The natural wrong answer is "yes", because 6 km is "tiny". The question gives the black-hole radius (the group proposal's version does too, so the learner must compare, not recall). Decision 5 discusses a three-button alternative.

**Prediction 2: how strong is gravity on its surface?** *"On the Earth's surface, gravity makes things fall faster by about 10 metres per second every second. On the surface of a neutron star of 1.4 Suns and 12 km radius, about how many times stronger is gravity?"* Buttons: **About 10 times**, **About a thousand times (10³)**, **About 100 billion times (10¹¹)**. Right answer: **about 100 billion times** (the model gives 1.5 × 10¹¹). Wrong answers are natural: a learner who thinks "dense, so strong" but without a sense of scale picks the first or second. The nearest-answer helper must use the log scale (Experiment 13's lesson).

- A neutron star of 1.418 Suns and 11.36 km has a black-hole radius of 4.19 km, a ratio of 2.71 and a compactness of 0.184.
