## Overview

The Earth holds gold, platinum and uranium, and none of it was made in the Sun, or in any ordinary star's fusion. The iron-and-lighter elements are made by fusion inside stars (Experiments 12 and 13). To build elements heavier than iron, the nuclei must **add neutrons faster than the new nuclei fall apart by radioactive decay**: this is the **rapid neutron-capture process, or "r-process"**. It needs a place with a huge number of free neutrons for a very short time. Which places do it, and how much each supplies, has been a puzzle for more than half a century.

The best-established answer is a **neutron star merger** (the collision of two neutron stars, Gravitational Waves Experiments 3 and 4 and Experiment 15 of this group). In August 2017 the gravitational-wave event GW170817 was followed by a glow, a **kilonova**, powered by the radioactive decay of freshly made heavy elements. LIGO's summary says about 10⁻² Suns of material was thrown out and that it contained gold and platinum, about 10 Earth masses of gold.

In April 2025 a team led by Anirudh Patel and Brian Metzger proposed a **second** source. A **magnetar** is a neutron star with an enormously strong magnetic field (Experiment 15 for neutron stars). A **giant flare** is the rare, huge outburst of such a star. On 27 December 2004 the magnetar SGR 1806−20 had one, about 8.7 kiloparsecs (about 28,000 light-years) away. The team argues that a flare shocks and throws out a piece of the star's crust at more than a tenth of the speed of light, that this material undergoes an r-process, and that the radioactive decay gives off gamma rays that match a faint, delayed "MeV" glow seen after the flare, which rose to a peak about 10 minutes after the first spike and faded over a few hours. They put the amount made at about 10⁻⁶ Suns, about a third of the Earth's mass, and say flares like it could supply about 1 to 10 percent of the Galaxy's r-process elements. Other 2026 papers assume the proposal in their own work. The Patel paper's authors state that the match does not exclude other explanations.

This experiment asks three questions:

1. **How do you make an element heavier than iron?** The learner sees what the r-process needs (neutrons added faster than they decay) and why it needs special places.
2. **How much does one event make?** One flare against one neutron star merger, drawn on one log mass scale against familiar masses (the Moon, the Earth).
3. **Could a rare event matter?** "Amount per event × how often" against what the Galaxy needs: the learner finds how often each kind of event would have to happen to supply all of the Galaxy's r-process material, and compares it with how often flares are actually seen.

The model is **counting**, not nuclear physics. It does not compute which elements are made. No new physics beyond what earlier experiments used (masses in Suns and Earths, rates per year, Experiment 15's merger context). What is new is the evidence and the habit of comparing sources by "amount per event × events per year".

## Learning Objective

By the end the learner can say, in their own words:

1. Elements heavier than iron need neutrons added faster than they decay (the r-process), which needs a very neutron-rich, violent place; ordinary fusion in stars does not do it.
2. Two sources are proposed: neutron star mergers (strong evidence, from the GW170817 kilonova) and magnetar giant flares (one reanalysis of 20-year-old data, contested).
3. One flare is estimated to make about 10⁻⁶ Suns (about 0.33 Earth masses, about 27 Moons) of r-process material; one merger like GW170817 threw out about 10⁻² Suns, about 30 times more material (and the sources say about 10 Earth masses of gold).
4. Total supply is amount per event × events per year. The Galaxy needs about 5 × 10⁻⁷ Suns of r-process material per year on average (the Patel paper's figure). A flare yields 10⁻⁶ Suns about once in 125 years, so about 8 × 10⁻⁹ Suns per year, about 1.6 percent of that need today, and the paper's 1 to 10 percent once the faster star formation of earlier times is allowed for.
5. A rare event can still matter if it makes a lot, and a more common event can matter little if it makes little: both numbers count.
6. How sure each source is: mergers are directly seen as a glow and are well established; the 2004 flare's glow is a match to a prediction, and other readings are not excluded; and the flare paper's own model is dominated by lighter nuclei, so "a flare made gold" is more than the paper says.

## Physical Situation

**A merger:** two neutron stars spiral together, and some of their matter, very rich in neutrons, is thrown out at about a tenth of the speed of light (NASA). The r-process happens in this cloud, and its radioactive decay lights it as a kilonova.

**A giant flare:** a magnetar's magnetic field rearranges, releasing about 10⁴⁶ erg in under a second (the paper). The shock heats and throws out a part of the star's crust. The model treats the flare as one event with one yield, and the merger likewise, as counting only.
