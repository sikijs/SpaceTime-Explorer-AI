## Overview

Gravity Experiment 2 showed that gravity changes the rate of a clock: a clock higher up, where gravity is a little weaker, ticks a little faster than one lower down. Gravity Experiment 9 showed that the effect is big enough to matter for GPS satellites, whose clocks are 20,000 kilometres up. This experiment asks the opposite question, at the smallest scale: **how small a height difference can the best clocks in the world now tell apart?**

In 2022 a team at JILA, a research institute in Boulder, Colorado, used a clock made of about 100,000 ultracold strontium atoms, held in a pattern of laser light called an **optical lattice**, and measured the rate of the clock at the top of the cloud and at the bottom of it, a height difference of **about one millimetre**, less than the width of a pencil tip. The clock at the top ticked faster, by about **one part in 10,000,000,000,000,000,000** (10⁻¹⁹), which is what general relativity predicts (computed: 1.09 × 10⁻¹⁹). A second team (Kolkowitz and colleagues, at the University of Wisconsin–Madison) measured the same effect across a height of one centimetre; their paper is dated 2022 and was revised and published in 2023. Clocks have also been carried out of the laboratory: a 2018 comparison of a transportable clock with another about 1,000 metres different in height, and a 2020 test between the foot and the 450-metre observatory floor of Tokyo Skytree, which showed in principle that a clock can tell a height (not yet a routine method). Optical clocks are also part of a plan to redefine the second.

This experiment lets the learner rebuild three ideas:

1. **A clock that is higher ticks faster, by an amount that grows in proportion to the height.** The fraction by which the rate differs is `g × h ÷ c²`, about 1.09 × 10⁻¹⁶ for each metre (computed), where g is the pull of gravity, h the height difference and c the speed of light.
2. **What a tiny rate difference adds up to.** For two clocks 1 metre apart, the gap after a year is about 3.4 nanoseconds (billionths of a second, computed); for 1 millimetre it is about 3.4 picoseconds (trillionths, computed).
3. **How good a clock must be to tell a height.** A clock that is good to one part in 10¹⁶ can tell heights about 0.9 metre apart; one good to 10⁻¹⁸ can tell about 9 millimetres; one good to 7.6 × 10⁻²¹ (the JILA clock's reported precision) could tell about 0.07 millimetre (computed). So a clock can be used to tell the height of the place it sits.

It says honestly what it does **not** do: it does **not** model an optical lattice clock or how it is made, it does **not** analyse the JILA or Wisconsin data, it does **not** model the Earth's real gravity field (it uses one constant g, which is a good approximation for heights of a few kilometres), and it does **not** say that the second *will* be redefined: that is a plan, not a result.

The new ideas are (1) **an optical lattice clock** and how a clock counts, (2) **a fractional rate difference** written as a power of ten, (3) **nanoseconds and picoseconds**, (4) **the height a clock can resolve**, and (5) **the plan to redefine the second**. "Everything falls alike" and "gravity slows clocks" are reused from Gravity Experiments 1, 2 and 9, and sigma from Experiments 1 and 2 of this group.

## Learning Objective

After this experiment, the learner should understand:

1. **A higher clock ticks faster.** The fraction is `g × h ÷ c²`, with g = 9.81 m/s² and c = 299,792,458 m/s: 1.09 × 10⁻¹⁶ per metre (computed).
2. **The effect is tiny but adds up.** A 1 m difference gives 3.4 nanoseconds after a year; 1 mm gives 3.4 picoseconds; a 4,300 m mountain gives about 14.8 microseconds (computed, with the constant-g approximation).
3. **The best clocks can see it across a millimetre.** In 2022 JILA reported a precision of 7.6 × 10⁻²¹ across a millimetre-scale sample, about 14 times smaller than the effect (1.09 × 10⁻¹⁹, computed). The Wisconsin team's 2022 to 2023 measurement across one centimetre agrees with the expected value (12.4 ± 0.7 ± 2.5, against 10.9, in units of 10⁻¹⁹ per centimetre; 0.58 sigma by this experiment's simplification, computed).
4. **How precise a clock must be to see a height.** The smallest height difference is `precision ÷ (g ÷ c²)`: 0.92 m for 10⁻¹⁶, 9.2 mm for 10⁻¹⁸, 0.07 mm for 7.6 × 10⁻²¹ (computed).
5. **What it is for, what has been shown and what is only planned.** Clocks that tell height could be used to compare heights across a country, or to measure the shape and gravity of the Earth (NIST's words); field tests in 2018 (about 1,000 m) and 2020 (450 m) showed in principle that a clock can tell a height, and it is not yet routine; and optical clocks are the candidates for a new definition of the second, which the 28th CGPM of October 2026 did not adopt, asking instead for a proposal in 2030.
6. **What the experiment did not show**: that the second has been redefined (it has not), or how an optical clock works inside.

## Physical Situation

### Reused
- **A clock higher up ticks faster** (Gravity Experiments 2 and 9): gravity slows clocks, a little less where gravity is weaker.
- **Sigma and compatibility** (Experiments 1, 2 and 8 of this group): how many give-or-takes a measurement is from an expected value.
- **The equivalence principle** (Gravity Experiment 1), as the reason that the effect exists (a clock "high up" behaves like a clock in an accelerating cabin that is "ahead").

### What is new
- **An optical lattice clock** (an outline only: atoms held in laser light, counted by the vibrations of light that they absorb).
- **A fractional rate difference**, written as a power of ten (10⁻¹⁶ means one part in ten thousand trillion), and **nanoseconds and picoseconds**.
- **The height a clock can resolve**, and the idea of using a clock to read a height (called, in the literature, chronometric geodesy).
- **The plan to redefine the second.**

### Simplifying assumptions (all stated to the learner in plain language)
- **One constant g, 9.81 m/s², and a flat surface.** The rate difference is `g × h ÷ c²`, the weak-field formula of Gravity Experiments 2 and 9. For heights up to a few kilometres this agrees with the exact formula to within about 0.2 percent (computed); for 100 kilometres it is about 1.4 percent off, and the page does not go above 10 kilometres.
- **The two clocks are identical, at rest, and compared exactly.** Real comparisons need links, corrections and a model of the Earth's gravity; the experiment says so and does not model them.
- **Precision is treated as the size of the smallest rate difference a clock can see** (a single number, as the papers quote it). It is not separated here into statistical and systematic parts, except in the Wisconsin result, where the two are combined in quadrature as in Experiment 8.
- **Published numbers are quoted as the papers state them.**

### Not introduced
How an optical clock works inside (cooling, trapping, the "magic wavelength", the clock laser), how clocks are compared over fibre or by satellite, quantum effects across the atom sample, dark matter searches with clocks, the Earth's real gravitational potential and the geoid, other clock species, and the details of the redefinition's criteria.

### The rules

- **Fractional rate difference:** `Δf ÷ f = g × h ÷ c²` (higher is faster), for a height difference h up to a few kilometres.
- **Time gap that builds up:** `Δt = (g × h ÷ c²) × T`, for a running time T.
- **Smallest height a clock can resolve:** `h = precision ÷ (g ÷ c²)`.
- **Sigma from the expected value (for the Wisconsin result):** `z = |measured − expected| ÷ √(statistical² + systematic²)`.
- **Signal against precision:** `(g × h ÷ c²) ÷ precision`.

### Introduction (as built; the owner approved the wording on 2026-10-21; the full text is in `ClockHeightExperiment.tsx`)

1. **The question.** "A clock sits on the floor and an identical clock sits on a shelf a metre higher. Do they tick at the same rate? Everyday sense says yes. But in Gravity Experiment 2 you saw that gravity changes the rate of a clock. Could the best clocks in the world tell how high they are by their tick alone?"
2. **Where we left off.** Gravity Experiments 1, 2 and 9 (everything falls alike; a higher clock ticks faster; GPS clocks need a correction) and Experiments 1, 2 and 8 of this group (sigma).
3. **What an atomic clock is.** Every clock counts something that repeats: a pendulum's swings, a quartz crystal's vibrations. An atomic clock counts something inside atoms: atoms of one kind absorb and give out light at a predictable frequency (the number of vibrations in a second), and the clock counts them. The second is defined today by caesium atoms, whose clocks count microwaves. An optical clock counts light itself: NIST says optical atomic clocks use light at frequencies "tens of thousands of times higher than the microwave frequencies emitted by cesium atoms", so they divide time into much finer pieces (a ruler marked in millimetres, against one marked in metres). An optical lattice clock holds thousands of ultracold atoms in a pattern of laser light, which NIST compares to eggs resting in an egg carton; JILA's held about 100,000 strontium atoms. A drawing (seen from the side) shows four layers of laser light with rows of atoms, the cloud's upper and lower edges, the light going to a counter, and gravity, each labelled, with a guide.
4. **Writing small numbers.** Powers of ten (10⁻³ is one part in a thousand; 10⁻¹⁹ is one part in ten million trillion), with the everyday scale from the module: a clock wrong by one part in 10¹⁹ would be wrong by one second in about 317 billion years, about 23 times the age of the universe (13.8 billion years), as a way to picture the size of the number. Milliseconds to picoseconds are a thousandth to a trillionth of a second; in a nanosecond light travels about 30 centimetres.
5. **The effect.** A higher clock ticks faster, and the higher it is the bigger the difference; the introduction says only that the difference is tiny and gives no number (that is what the predictions ask).
6. **The measurements.** JILA (2022) and the University of Wisconsin–Madison team (published 2023) are named, without their heights or numbers; the 2018 field test (a transportable clock against a clock about 1,000 metres different in height; "a proof of principle, and not yet routine") and the 2020 Tokyo Skytree test (450 metres) are named too.
7. **Why it matters.** NIST says that ultraprecise clocks could also help to measure the shape and gravity of the Earth; and optical clocks are candidates for a new definition of the second, which is a plan, not a result: on 13 to 15 October 2026 the 28th CGPM did not adopt a new definition, asked for a proposal in 2030, and noted that there is no consensus on which atom or atoms to use.
8. **The state of the question, as of October 2026.** A neutral line before the run (laboratories have measured the effect with optical lattice clocks, and clocks have been carried out of the laboratory for field tests; the full status is shown after the run).
