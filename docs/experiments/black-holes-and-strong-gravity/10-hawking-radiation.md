## Overview

Every experiment so far treated a black hole as a one-way door: things go in, nothing comes out. Experiment 9 showed how one is born. This experiment asks about its end: **can a black hole shrink and disappear?**

In 1974 Stephen Hawking showed, by combining quantum physics with gravity, that a black hole is not perfectly black. It gives off a very faint glow of heat, called **Hawking radiation**, and the energy in that glow comes out of the black hole's mass, so the black hole slowly loses mass. The experiment gives two results, both stated and not derived:

1. **A black hole has a temperature, and a small black hole is hotter than a big one.** `T = ħc³ ÷ (8πGMk_B)`: double the mass and the temperature halves. For 1 Sun it is about 0.00000006 K (6 × 10⁻⁸ K), far colder than anything we can measure; for a mountain's mass it is about 10¹¹ K.
2. **Whether a black hole loses mass or gains it depends on its surroundings.** A hot thing in a cooler place loses heat, and a cold thing in a warmer place gains it. Space is not empty of heat: it is filled with the **cosmic microwave background** at 2.725 K (Cosmology, Experiment 3). Every black hole of 0.6 Moon masses or more is *colder* than that, so today it takes in more than it gives out. It will start to shrink only when the background has cooled below the black hole's own temperature.

The learner picks a black hole from a short list, from a mountain-sized one up to M87\*, and runs the experiment. A thermometer-style picture compares the black hole's temperature with the background's. A second picture shows which way the black hole's mass is going. A graph shows how long it would take to evaporate, if it were alone and the background were gone, set against the age of the universe.

The experiment is a **thought experiment** about a lone black hole in a bath of background heat. Hawking radiation has never been observed from a real black hole (only imitations in laboratory systems), and the experiment says so plainly.

## Learning Objective

After this experiment, the learner should understand:

1. **A black hole is not perfectly black: it glows very faintly.** This is a result of combining quantum physics with gravity (Hawking, 1974). It is stated, not derived. The popular picture of "a pair of particles appears at the edge, one falls in and one escapes" is only a rough guide, and Hawking himself said so.
2. **A black hole has a temperature, and it goes down as the mass goes up.** For 1 Sun: about 6 × 10⁻⁸ K. For 10 Suns: 6 × 10⁻⁹ K (ten times colder). For Sagittarius A\*: about 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ K. A small black hole is much hotter: a mountain's mass (10¹² kg) is about 10¹¹ K.
3. **The glow carries energy away, so the black hole loses mass.** The smaller it gets, the hotter it gets, and the faster it loses mass: an unfeeding black hole would end in an ever-faster fade.
4. **Heat flows from hot to cold, so the black hole's surroundings decide.** A black hole colder than the 2.725 K background takes in more heat than it gives out and gains mass; one hotter than the background loses mass. The balance point is a mass of about 4.5 × 10²² kg, about 0.6 of the Moon's mass (a horizon radius of about 0.07 mm). Every black hole found in space is far heavier than that, so today every one of them is gaining, not losing, mass (by a tiny amount).
5. **The very long time scale.** If a black hole were alone in a perfectly cold space, a black hole of 1 Sun would take about 2 × 10⁶⁷ years to evaporate; 10 Suns, about 2 × 10⁷⁰; Sagittarius A\*, about 2 × 10⁸⁷; M87\*, about 6 × 10⁹⁶. The universe is about 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years old. The time grows as the *cube* of the mass: ten times the mass takes a thousand times as long. A black hole that is evaporating just now, after the universe's whole age, would have started with about 1.7 × 10¹¹ kg, the mass of a small mountain.
6. **What is settled and what is not.** The formula is a standard result; the radiation has never been seen from a real black hole; and what happens at the very end, and to the information that fell in (the "information paradox"), is not settled (named in one sentence).
7. What the experiment does not say: how a black hole is fed (gas, stars, mergers), how fast it gains mass from the background (it is far too small to matter), how the radiation is derived, the exact spectrum of the glow, and what the end of the evaporation looks like.

## Physical Situation

A single non-spinning black hole of a chosen mass `M`, alone, in a bath of the cosmic microwave background at today's temperature, 2.725 K. The experiment asks how hot the black hole is, whether it is hotter or colder than the background, and how long it would take to evaporate with no background and nothing falling in.

### The statements the model uses (given, not derived)

All are standard results, stated here and not derived (deriving them needs quantum field theory in curved spacetime). They were checked on 2026-10-12 against search results (Wikipedia-type pages and arXiv papers) and against Claude's own arithmetic from the CODATA constants, and not against the original papers.

- **The black hole's temperature (Hawking temperature):** `T = ħc³ ÷ (8πGMk_B)` ≈ **6.17 × 10⁻⁸ K ÷ (M ÷ 1 Sun)**. It is inversely proportional to the mass.
- **The lifetime, with no background and nothing falling in:** `t = 5120πG²M³ ÷ (ħc⁴)` ≈ **2.10 × 10⁶⁷ years × (M ÷ 1 Sun)³**. This is the standard simplest form, and it ignores two things that change it by a factor of a few: that the real black hole does not radiate like a perfect glowing body (the "greybody factor"), and the other kinds of particle that a hot small black hole can give off. The experiment says it is an order-of-magnitude statement and shows it on a log scale.
- **The background's temperature:** 2.725 K, **reused unchanged** from the Cosmic Microwave Background experiment (`CMB_TEMPERATURE_TODAY_KELVIN`).
- **Which way the mass goes:** decided by comparing the black hole's temperature with the background's, which is a standard result of thermal balance (a body hotter than its surroundings loses heat, a colder one gains it). **The experiment does not compute how fast** the black hole gains mass from the background, because that needs the greybody factors; it gives the direction only (Decision 3).
- **The balance mass:** `M_eq = ħc³ ÷ (8πGk_B × 2.725 K)` ≈ **4.50 × 10²² kg**, about 0.61 Moon masses.
- **The horizon radius:** `r_s = 2GM/c²`, **reused from Experiment 1** (`horizonRadiusKm`).
- **The universe's age:** about 1.38 × 10¹⁰ years, the value the Cosmology experiments already use (to be reused from their module if one defines it; otherwise restated and flagged).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Balance mass | 4.5 × 10²² kg | 2.725 K | equal | balanced | 2.4 × 10⁴⁴ years |
| 1 Sun | 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg | 6.2 × 10⁻⁸ K | 44 million times colder | gains | 2.1 × 10⁶⁷ years |

(For the Sun's black hole to begin to lose mass, the background would have to cool by a factor of about 44 million, from 2.725 K to below 6.2 × 10⁻⁸ K.) The mass of a black hole whose lifetime is the age of the universe is about 1.7 × 10¹¹ kg.

### Simplifying assumptions (must be stated to the learner, in plain language)

- **A thought experiment about one lone, non-spinning black hole** that nothing falls into.
- **The formulas are standard results, given and not derived.** The glow has never been seen from a real black hole, and the experiment says so.
- **Lifetimes are order-of-magnitude values** (the simplest form of the formula, no greybody factor, no other kinds of particle).
- **The background is held at 2.725 K.** The real background keeps cooling as the universe expands (Cosmology, Experiment 3), and the experiment does not model when it would drop below a black hole's temperature (Decision 4).
- **Direction only, not rate,** for a black hole in the background.
- **The experiment stops before the last stage** of an evaporation, which is not understood.

### Not introduced

The derivation of the radiation, quantum field theory, the particle-pair picture beyond one sentence calling it a rough guide, the exact shape of the glow's spectrum, the greybody factor beyond naming it as a reason the lifetimes are rough, the information paradox beyond one sentence, spinning and charged black holes' temperatures, primordial black holes (named only in passing, as the small black holes the mountain-sized preset stands for; none has been found), how fast the background feeds a black hole, the far future of the universe (Cosmology, Experiment 10), and any black hole imitations in laboratories. All explicitly out of scope (`CLAUDE.md` §16).

## Physics Model

One new, self-contained module, in the style of Experiments 1 to 9. It **reuses** earlier code unchanged (imported, not redefined): `horizonRadiusKm` and the Sagittarius A\* and M87\* masses from Experiment 1 (`blackHoleSizeExperiment.ts`), and `CMB_TEMPERATURE_TODAY_KELVIN` from `cosmicMicrowaveBackgroundExperiment.ts`. A regression test checks that it imports nothing else from another experiment.

```typescript
// src/physics/hawkingRadiationExperiment.ts

export const HAWKING_TEMPERATURE_KELVIN_PER_SOLAR_MASS_INVERSE = 6.17e-8  // T of a 1-Sun hole; derived from constants and tested against them
export const UNIVERSE_AGE_YEARS = 1.38e10

export type MassDirection = 'loses' | 'balanced' | 'gains'

export interface HawkingPreset { id: string; label: string; massKg: number }

export function hawkingTemperatureKelvin(massKg: number): number
export function lifetimeYears(massKg: number): number           // alone, no background
export function balanceMassKg(): number                         // temperature equals the background's
export function massDirection(massKg: number): MassDirection    // compares T with the background
export function massForLifetimeKg(years: number): number        // inverse of lifetimeYears

export interface HawkingResult {
  massKg: number
  massSolarMasses: number
  horizonRadiusKm: number                // reused from Experiment 1
  temperatureKelvin: number
  backgroundKelvin: number               // reused
  temperatureOverBackground: number      // > 1: hotter, < 1: colder
  direction: MassDirection
  lifetimeYears: number
  lifetimeOverUniverseAge: number
  coolingFactorNeeded: number | null     // how many times colder the background must get for the hole to start losing mass; null if it already would
  curve: Array<{ massKg: number; temperatureKelvin: number; lifetimeYears: number }>
}

export function runHawkingRadiationExperiment(massKg: number): HawkingResult
```

The constants (ħ, c, G, k_B, the Sun's mass, the length of a year) are written in the module with their sources. The temperature and lifetime are written once, from the constants, and the "6.17 × 10⁻⁸ K for 1 Sun" and "2.10 × 10⁶⁷ years" figures are *tested* against them, not typed in as the source. Structured independently of the UI (`CLAUDE.md` §9): pure functions, testable with no browser. The playback is a fixed real duration and cannot change a result (`CLAUDE.md` §11).

## Prediction Activity

Before running, the learner answers three questions, all required before Run is enabled (the same gating as Experiments 5 to 9). Wording is a draft for the owner's review (§28.1):

1. "A black hole is huge, and a small one is tiny. Which do you think would be hotter, if they glowed with heat?" (The big one / The small one / Both the same.) The model's answer is the second (`T ∝ 1/M`).
2. "Black holes in space are surrounded by the cold, faint heat of the cosmic microwave background, at 2.725 K, about 270 degrees below freezing. A black hole of 10 Suns glows at a temperature far below that. Do you think it is losing mass, gaining mass, or staying the same?" (Losing / Gaining / The same.) The model's answer is gaining. Note: the question tells the learner the temperature is far below the background's; it does not give the direction (Decision 5, the wording to be confirmed).
3. "If a black hole had ten times the mass, how much longer do you think it would take to evaporate, if it were alone?" (About 10 times as long / About 100 times as long / About 1,000 times as long.) The model's answer is the third (`t ∝ M³`).

Nothing about the answers is shown before Run (§12). The experiment's introduction must not state that small black holes are hotter, that a black hole can be colder than the background, or the lifetime's dependence on mass.

## Decisions Needing Human Review

Each has Claude's recommended default; the owner may change any.

1. **One idea, and what it is.** Default: one idea, "a black hole has a temperature that falls as the mass rises, and whether it loses or gains mass depends on the surroundings", with the lifetime as a third picture of the same formula (as the energy was a second, shared idea in Experiment 8). Alternative: drop Picture 3 and the lifetime, and keep only the temperature and the direction, which is simpler but loses the "very long time scale" and the link to the universe's age.
2. **The black holes offered.** Default: mountain-sized (10¹² kg), the balance mass (4.5 × 10²² kg), 1 Sun, 10 Suns, Sagittarius A\* and M87\*, so that one is hotter than the background, one is equal, and four are colder, and all reuse Experiment 1's names. Alternative: a free slider for the mass (as in Cosmology, Experiment 8).
3. **Direction only, not the rate of gain.** Default: the experiment says which way the mass goes and gives no rate for a black hole in the background, because a correct rate needs the greybody factors, which are beyond the "stated, not derived" level. Alternative: add a rate using the simple glowing-body law, which would look more precise than the physics here allows.
4. **The background's cooling.** Default: hold the background at 2.725 K and say only that it keeps cooling and that the experiment does not say when it would fall below a black hole's temperature, since that needs the far future of the universe, which Cosmology Experiment 10's specification says was not researched. Alternative: add a later question, "when would the background reach 6 × 10⁻⁸ K?", as a second experiment or a Cosmology addition; it needs a researched number and is out of scope here.
5. **How to avoid giving the answers away.** Default: the title and the group overview line must still name Hawking radiation (the title is the topic), but the introduction avoids saying that black holes are hot, that small ones are hotter, or that they can be colder than the background; Prediction 2 gives the learner the temperature comparison (far colder) and asks only what that means for the direction, which tests whether the learner can apply "hot to cold" to a surprising object. Alternative: Prediction 2 asks which way a *small mountain-sized* black hole's mass goes instead, which is less of a surprise.
6. **Daily-life comparisons.** Default: for "heat flows from hot to cold and the surroundings decide", a cup of coffee in a cold room (it cools) and a cold drink in a warm room (it warms); for the cube law, a cube of side 10 cm against one of side 1 m (ten times the side, a thousand times the volume), to be confirmed by the owner (§15, §28.1). Neither relies on relativity. The "small is hotter" idea has no good everyday comparison, and the tutor says so plainly rather than offering a bad one.
7. **The 1974 date, and Hawking's name.** Default: name them as general knowledge that matches the search results, flagged for the owner. Alternative: leave the date out.
8. **Primordial black holes.** Default: the mountain-sized preset is described as "a black hole of the mass of a small mountain, such as some have suggested could have formed in the early universe; none has been found", in one sentence, flagged as not checked against a primary source. Alternative: describe it only as "a hypothetical small black hole".
9. **Not checked against a primary source:** the temperature and lifetime formulas, the 4.50 × 10²² kg balance mass and the 1.7 × 10¹¹ kg evaporating-now mass (own arithmetic from CODATA constants, checked against search results, not Hawking's paper or a textbook); that Hawking radiation has never been observed from a real black hole (Wikipedia-type pages and arXiv papers); the information paradox being unsettled; the masses of Sagittarius A\* and M87\* (reused from Experiment 1); the 1974 date; and the primordial-black-hole sentence. Claude recommends a textbook check of the two formulas before the physics step, as was done for Experiment 9's formula.
