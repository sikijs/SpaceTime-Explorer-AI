## Introduction (above Part 1, no Part heading)

### 1. The puzzle

Our galaxy, the Milky Way, holds a few hundred billion stars and is more than 100,000 light-years across. (A light-year is the distance light travels in a year, nearly 10 trillion kilometres.) Its stars together shine with the light of about 20 to 30 billion Suns.

Now picture a single point of light, so small it looks like one star in a telescope, that shines brighter than all of those stars put together. It sits in the middle of a galaxy billions of light-years away. Astronomers call such an object a **quasar**. This page asks two questions about one: **where does the energy come from, and how small can the thing that makes it be?**

### 2. What a quasar looks like

Through an ordinary telescope a quasar looks like a star: a sharp point, not a fuzzy cloud. That is where its name comes from: "quasar" is short for "quasi-stellar", meaning "star-like". [recheck: name credited to Hong-yee Chiu, 1964, Caltech page.] The galaxy around it is there, but the quasar's glare drowns it out, a little like trying to see the car behind a headlight while looking straight into the beam.

*Image 1 (NASA, ESA, Bin Ren, 2024): the quasar 3C 273 with its glare blocked so the galaxy around it shows. Alt text to be written after the image is viewed.*

### 3. The first quasar

In 1963 the astronomer Maarten Schmidt studied the light of a faint "star" called 3C 273 and found its colours were strange. They made sense only if the object was moving away from us extremely fast, which in an expanding universe means it is extremely far away. The Chandra Field Guide says the spectrum "made sense if it was simply an extremely large redshift" [recheck].

*Redshift, in plain words:* the light of a source that is moving away is stretched toward red, the way a siren's pitch drops as it drives away. The only idea needed here: **a large redshift tells us the source is very far away.**

NASA's 2024 Hubble release gives 3C 273's distance as 2.5 billion light-years. Older pages say 2 billion or 3 billion; this page uses NASA's newer figure. To look as bright as it does from that far, it must give out an enormous amount of light. Sources disagree on how much (about 2, 3.6 or 5 trillion Suns), so **this page says "about 4 trillion Suns, and the sources differ".**

*Image 3 (ESA/Hubble & NASA, 2013): 3C 273 and its jet.*

### 4. What feeds it

The accepted explanation is a **supermassive black hole**, millions to billions of times the Sun's mass, at the galaxy's centre. Gas falling toward it does not drop straight in. It swirls into a flat, hot disk (the **accretion disk**) and glows just before it goes in. A black hole does not shine; **the glowing gas just outside it does.**

Black Holes 8 found that a probe spiralling to a black hole's last safe orbit gives up between about 4 and 42 percent of its rest-mass energy, depending on the spin, and said where that energy goes was not modelled. For a quasar, a large part of it comes out as light.

*Image 4 (ESO/L. Calçada, 2015): an artist's impression of a black hole, its disk and jets. A drawing, not a photograph.*

### 5. The two halves of the puzzle (stated, not answered)

- **Energy.** How much gas must fall in each year to make that much light?
- **Size.** If the light flickers, how big can the thing making it be?

You will guess three numbers before anything runs.

### 6. How the size limit works (the idea, no numbers yet)

Picture a stadium crowd doing "the wave". It takes time to go round, because each person reacts only after seeing their neighbour. Now suppose the whole stadium lit up and went dark together in one second. That could only happen if no part were farther from another than light travels in one second, because no part can learn of a change elsewhere faster than that. The same holds for a quasar: **if its light brightens and fades as a whole in a time T, it cannot be wider than the distance light covers in time T.** A review by Peterson gives a quasar that "doubles in a week" as an example [recheck]; this page uses a one-week flicker as an **illustration**, not as 3C 273's own measured flicker.

*Image 5 (NASA/CXC/SAO/H. Marshall et al., 2000): the X-ray jet of 3C 273.*

### 7. A record-breaker (the latest, as of November 2026)

In February 2024 ESO announced J0529-4351: "over 500 trillion times more luminous than the Sun", with a black hole of 17 billion Suns that "eats just over a Sun per day", and light that took over 12 billion years to reach us [recheck all against eso2402]. It was catalogued as a star for decades because it was too bright to be taken for a quasar. These are the discoverers' measurements and estimates, not this page's.

### 8. Still being learned (each is that source's claim, with its date)

- NASA/Chandra, 18 September 2025: a quasar, RACS J0320-35, whose black hole is estimated to grow by 300 to 3,000 Suns a year, 12.8 billion light-years away [recheck].
- ESA/Webb, 19 November 2025: a feasting black hole less than 600 million years after the Big Bang [recheck].

### 9. What this page does and does not do

It uses two ideas, energy and size. **It does not model** how the disk turns falling gas into light, what makes the jets, dust, the galaxy's own light, how the black hole is fed, how long a quasar lasts, or the Eddington limit (the learner's Cosmic Horizons 22, specification 23, takes up how fast a black hole can grow). It assumes that the energy share Black Holes 8 computed for a probe is a fair stand-in for the share of falling gas turned to light: up to about 42 percent in theory, while some textbooks quote about 32 percent for the most extreme realistic case [recheck OpenStax].

**The predictions never change the real pictures or tables.** Your guesses are drawn on them in white and shown again beside the results. In "Now try it yourself", only the settings change Picture C.

## "Words and formulas" (collapsed box)

- **Galaxy**: a huge family of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity. The Milky Way is one.
- **Quasar**: the extremely bright centre of a distant galaxy, powered by gas falling into a supermassive black hole.
- **Luminosity**: how much light something gives out each second, counted here in Suns. "4 trillion Suns" is 4 trillion times the Sun's output (3.828 × 10²⁶ watts, IAU 2015).
- **Redshift**: the stretching of light from a faraway source toward red; a larger redshift means farther away.
- **Accretion disk**: the flat, swirling, hot disk of gas that spirals toward a black hole.
- **Efficiency**: the share of a mass that comes out as energy. **Energy = efficiency × mass × c².** Worked example: at 5.72 percent, 1 kg gives 0.0572 × 1 kg × (299,792,458 m/s)² ≈ 5.1 × 10¹⁵ joules.
- **Light-day**: the distance light travels in a day, 25.9 billion km or about 173 AU (1 AU is the Earth–Sun distance). A light-week is 7 of them.
- **Flicker**: a change in brightness over time. **Size limit: width ≤ c × time.** Worked example: 7 days gives 7 light-days, about 1,212 AU.
- **Mass each year**: luminosity ÷ (efficiency × c²), the number Picture C shows.
