## Overview

For the first few minutes the whole universe was hot and dense enough for nuclear reactions, like the inside of a star. In those minutes, the **neutrons** that were around either decayed or got locked into **helium** nuclei, and almost none became heavier elements. That fixes the recipe of ordinary matter before any star shone: about three-quarters hydrogen and one-quarter helium by mass, with traces of deuterium and lithium. It is the oldest test of the Big Bang that does not rely on the microwave background.

1. **The neutron share.** Neutrons are a little heavier than protons (1.293 MeV, summary-only source). While the universe was hot, collisions turned neutrons into protons and back about equally; as it cooled these stopped, and the neutron share froze at a value set by a "weight" factor. Then free neutrons decayed (lifetime 878.4 s, summary-only source).
2. **Locking into helium.** About three minutes in, deuterium (one proton plus one neutron) could finally survive, and nearly every neutron ended up in a helium-4 nucleus (2 protons plus 2 neutrons). So the helium share by mass is 2x ÷ (1 + x), where x is neutrons per proton (**formula from the arithmetic of counting, not read from a source**).
3. **The tests.** Observed helium 0.2458 ± 0.0013 against a published prediction of 0.2467 ± 0.0002 (2026 paper, abstract-level read); deuterium 2.508 ± 0.029 against 2.445 ± 0.038 (×10⁻⁵, summary-only); lithium-7 1.45 ± 0.25 against 5.52 ± 0.20 (×10⁻¹⁰, summary-only).
4. **The odd one out.** Helium and deuterium agree; lithium-7 does not. The page says plainly that nobody has settled why.

Status: **Settled** for helium and deuterium (the predicted and observed values agree within about 1 sigma and 2 percent); **Open** for lithium (a factor of about 3). Said as an old and well-tested result with one unsolved part, not as a new discovery. "As of October 2026."

## Learning Objective

By the end the learner can say, in their own words:

1. The early universe made its own light elements in the first few minutes, mostly hydrogen and helium, before any star existed.
2. About a quarter of ordinary matter by mass is helium, and why: it is set by how many neutrons were left when helium could first form.
3. By number of atoms that quarter is only about 8 helium atoms per 100 hydrogen atoms, because a helium atom is four times heavier than a hydrogen atom.
4. Waiting longer would have meant fewer neutrons and less helium (neutrons decay), so the recipe depends on timing.
5. Helium and deuterium match the measurements, and lithium does not: what has been tested, and what is still open.
6. What the page's model leaves out, and which numbers are calculated here and which are quoted.

## Physical Situation

The early universe in the first minutes, treated as a mixture of protons and neutrons in the Big Bang's hot, expanding gas, in the lab-at-rest frame of the expanding universe. Time is measured from the start of the expansion. No star, no galaxy, no dark matter or dark energy matters to this experiment.
