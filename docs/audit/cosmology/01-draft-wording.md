## 1. Introduction (above Part 1; no answers given, §12)

**Title:** Cosmology 1: Hubble's Law

**The question.** "When astronomers look at galaxies far beyond our own, they find something odd about their light. Does it depend on how far away the galaxy is? And does a galaxy's distance tell us how fast it is moving away from us?"

**What happens.** (Rewritten 2026-11-14 at the owner's request: "more detailed ... explain redshift, the pattern, and the number that sets the pattern's size". Four short numbered steps.)
1. "**Light is a wave, and its colour is the length of that wave.** Think of ripples on a pond. The distance from one ripple's peak to the next is its **wavelength**. Light works the same way, only far smaller. Blue light has short waves and red light has long waves. Light from a distant galaxy travels for millions of years before it reaches us."
2. "**What redshift is.** On that long journey the light's waves get **stretched**, so the light arrives with longer waves than it left with, which means it arrives **redder**. That is called **redshift**. It is like stretching a spring: the coils pull apart. **Why?** Because **space itself is expanding**. Picture a wave drawn on a rubber band that someone slowly pulls longer: the wave is not changed, but the band under it grows, so the gap between its peaks grows too. Light crossing the expanding space is stretched the same way, a little more every moment of its journey. (This is not the same as a galaxy moving through space, which can also change a wave's length. The tutor explains the difference at the end.) Astronomers can tell how much the light was stretched because atoms give out light in a recognisable pattern of lines at known colours, like a fingerprint. If the lines turn up at longer wavelengths than the fingerprint says, the light was stretched. The amount is written as a number, **z**: the fraction by which the wavelength grew. If z is 0.01, a wavelength of 500 nanometres arrives as 505. A bigger z means more stretching."
3. "**What 'the pattern' means.** Astronomers can measure two things for a galaxy: its redshift, and how far away it is (for example, from how dim a star of known brightness looks). A **pattern** here means a rule that links the two, so that knowing one tells you something about the other. Edwin Hubble published such a rule in 1929, and it is called **Hubble's Law**. What the rule says is what you are about to find out, so it is not stated yet."
4. "**What 'the number that sets the pattern's size' means.** Take a taxi fare as an example. The fare depends on how far you go, and the price per kilometre is the number that says how much the fare changes for each extra kilometre. A different price per kilometre gives a different fare for the same trip, even though the rule is the same. In Hubble's Law the matching number is the **Hubble constant**. It is written in **km/s per Mpc**: it tells how much a galaxy's speed away from us changes for each extra megaparsec of distance. This page uses 70. The real value is still debated (see the check-up in Part 3)."
Then: "Studying what this pattern says about the whole universe is called **cosmology**."

It does not say whether it grows or by how much, but it does tell the learner that speed depends on distance; the introduction's question already asks that. Say if you want it softer.

**Words first (§15).**
- "**Light-year**: the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 million million kilometres."
- "**Megaparsec (Mpc)**: a distance astronomers use for galaxies. One Mpc is about 3.3 million light-years. A galaxy 16.5 Mpc away is about 54 million light-years away."
- "**Recession speed**: how fast a galaxy is moving away from us, in kilometres per second (km/s). For comparison, a fast jet flies at about 0.3 km/s."
- "**Redshift (z)**: how much the light's wavelength has been stretched, as a fraction. If z is 0.01, a wavelength of 500 nanometres arrives as 505. Redder light has a longer wavelength."
- "**Wavelength**: the distance between one peak of a light wave and the next. Blue light has a shorter wavelength than red light."

**What we assume.** "(1) The redshift shown here is explained by **space itself stretching** while the light is on its way, not by a galaxy rushing through space the way the gravitational-wave source did in Gravitational Waves Experiment 5 (that kind of stretching is called the Doppler effect). (2) The simple rule this page uses to work out a galaxy's redshift from its distance is only accurate for galaxies that are not too far away. At much larger distances the real relationship is more complicated, and it is not covered here. The rule uses three ingredients: the galaxy's **distance** (written **d**), the **Hubble constant** (written **H0**, 70 km/s per Mpc here) and the **speed of light** (written **c**, 299,792 km/s). It gives the **redshift** (written **z**). How they combine appears in the Words and formulas box after your first run. *[Changed 2026-11-14 at the owner's question: the formula is held back until after Run because it gives away both guesses (§12). The introduction names the four ingredients and their symbols without showing how they combine (the owner's option 3, 2026-11-14), and the symbols are defined again with the formula after Run.]* (3) The Hubble constant is set to **70 km/s per Mpc**, a round value chosen for this page. Its real value is still debated (see the check-up in Part 3). (4) The galaxies are not simulated, apart from a distance. (5) Only galaxies moving away are shown; a few nearby galaxies move toward us because of local gravity, and that is not modelled."

## 2. Words and formulas (collapsed box above Part 1)
- **Light-year, megaparsec, recession speed, redshift, wavelength:** as above.
- **Hubble's Law:** held back from the box until after the Run (it answers both guesses). Shown in the tutor's explanation, and in the box once the Run has finished: "Hubble's Law: a galaxy's recession speed is the Hubble constant times its distance, v = H0 × d."
- **Hubble constant (H0):** "A number that tells how much recession speed there is for each megaparsec of distance. Written in km/s per Mpc. This page uses 70."
- **Cosmology:** "The study of the universe as a whole: its beginning, its size, how it changes."
- **Doppler effect:** "The change in a wave's wavelength caused by its source moving through space, as when an ambulance siren drops in pitch as it passes. The redshift on this page has a different cause."
- **Hubble tension:** "A real disagreement between two ways of measuring the Hubble constant, which give about 73 and about 67 km/s per Mpc (see Part 3)."
- **Symbols (added 2026-11-14), shown with the formulas after the run:** "z is the redshift. H0 is the Hubble constant (70 km/s per Mpc here). d is the galaxy's distance in Mpc. v is its recession speed in km/s. c is the speed of light, 299,792 km/s." The "Hubble's Law" entry and the "Where the real picture comes from" line carry a bracket naming the symbols too.
- **Formulas, with a worked example from the page's own numbers (arithmetic checked against the module by hand; to be put in a test):** v = H0 × d = 70 × 16.5 = **1,155 km/s**. z = v ÷ c = 1,155 ÷ 299,792.458 = **0.00385**. Stretch factor = 1 + z = **1.00385**; a wavelength of 500 nm arrives as 500 × 1.00385 = 501.9 nm. "c is the speed of light, 299,792 km/s."

**Where the real picture comes from.**
- **Calculated here:** each galaxy's recession speed (70 km/s per Mpc × its distance), its redshift z = H0 × d ÷ c, and the stretch of its light.
- **Measured:** the Virgo Cluster's distance, 16.5 Mpc (Mei and others, 2007). Galaxy A's distance of 33 Mpc is not a real galaxy.
- **Imagined for the question:** the light's colour and the wave's width, drawn **much stronger than the real stretch** so that you can see them (z of 0.05 is drawn as fully red; the real z here is about 0.004 to 0.008), and Galaxy A itself.

### Caption: How to read this diagram
**What is drawn.**
- **Two rows.** The top row is Galaxy B and the bottom row Galaxy A. Each has a galaxy on the left, Earth on the right, a dashed line for the path of the light, and a wavy line for the light itself. The farther a galaxy is from Earth, the farther left it is drawn.
- **The light wave and its dot.** The wavy line is the light, and the small dot is its leading edge. It starts as pale blue-white, the light's colour when it left the galaxy. The wave's colour and the spacing between its peaks show how much it has been stretched so far.
- **The ring round Earth.** Its colour shows how stretched the light was when it arrived.
- **The Recession speed panel.** One horizontal bar for each galaxy. A longer bar means a faster galaxy. A missing bar means that galaxy has not been run yet. The scale is in km/s (kilometres per second).
- **White marks (your guesses).** Drawn only after the light has arrived. The white bar is the length A's bar would have if your guess about speed were right. The white marker is on the row whose light you guessed would look redder, or between the rows for "no difference". It is only a marker, with no size of its own. **They show your guess. They do not say whether it is right.**

**What appears when.** (1) The galaxies, Earth and labels. (2) After Run, the light wave travels from each galaxy to Earth and changes colour on the way. (3) When it arrives, the rings, the speed bars and your white marks appear.

**A worked moment.** "Galaxy B is 16.5 Mpc away. Its speed is 70 × 16.5 = 1,155 km/s. Its redshift is 1,155 ÷ 299,792 = 0.00385, so a 500 nm wavelength arrives as about 501.9 nm. Galaxy A is 33 Mpc away. Its speed is 70 × 33 = 2,310 km/s and z = 0.00771."

**What to look for** (each shown only after its feature is drawn):
1. **Look at:** the two speed bars. **What it means:** B's bar ends at 1,155 km/s and A's at 2,310 km/s; they are the speeds worked out for those two distances. **Why it matters:** it is the number your first guess was about, so you can compare the white bar with the real one.
2. **Look at:** the two rings round Earth. **What it means:** each ring's colour is how stretched that galaxy's light was on arrival (z = 0.00385 for B, 0.00771 for A). **Why it matters:** it is what your second guess was about, so you can compare the white marker with the real rings.
3. **Look at:** the spacing between the peaks of the light waves near Earth and near the galaxy. **What it means:** peaks start close together and spread out on the journey; the stretching builds up during the trip, not at the start. **Why it matters:** it shows the stretching happens to the light while it travels. The full reason is for the tutor.

"The colour and width are drawn much stronger than the real stretch. The exact numbers are in the table in Part 3."
