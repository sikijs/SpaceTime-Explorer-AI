## 1. Introduction (above Part 1; no answers given, §12)

**Title:** Cosmology 2: The Big Bang

**The question.** "In Cosmology 1 you found a rule linking a galaxy's distance to how fast it is moving away from us (Hubble's Law). If every distant galaxy is moving away, what happens if we run that backward in time?"

**What happens.** "Running the rule backward lets us ask, for any galaxy, how long ago its distance from us would have been zero, if it had always moved at its present speed. That moment is popularly called **the Big Bang**, and the time back to it is called the **Hubble time**. Physicists call this starting moment a **singularity**: not a place you could travel to or examine, but the point where this simplified model's distances and timeline both reach zero. What actually happens there needs physics (quantum gravity) that this experiment does not model."

**What the Big Bang was not (added 2026-11-14 at the owner's request).** "Almost everyone has heard of the Big Bang, but the name is misleading. It was not an explosion in one place, with empty space around it for the blast to spread into, and the universe has no centre. A better picture is **space itself stretching**, the same stretching you met in Cosmology 1. NASA's John Mather, a Nobel laureate, calls 'Big Bang' 'a really misleading name for the expanding universe that we see' (see the links)." *(Sourced to NASA's Webb Q&A with Mather, read in full. The phrase "everywhere at once" is deliberately left to the tutor: it hints at guess 1's answer.)*

**A puzzle the Big Bang has to answer: why does the whole sky match? (reinstated 2026-11-14 at the owner's request; replaces the removed horizon-problem paragraph).**
1. "**The glow.** Look at the faint glow that fills the whole sky, left over from the early universe. NASA calls it the universe's 'baby picture': light that was set free about 380,000 years after the beginning. Point a telescope one way, then point it the exact opposite way, and the glow has almost exactly the same temperature in both. The differences are tiny."
2. "**Why that is strange.** Think of two cups of tea. They only end up at the same temperature if heat can pass between them, or if they were poured from the same pot. For two parts of space to match, something has to pass between them, such as heat or light. And nothing travels faster than light."
3. "**The catch.** When that glow was set free, the universe was only about 380,000 years old, so light had only had 380,000 years to travel. But the two patches of sky we are looking at were already far, far farther apart than that. Light from one could never have reached the other. They had never been in touch, never swapped heat or light, and yet they match."
4. "**A picture of it.** Imagine two strangers who have never met, never phoned and never seen each other, and who turn up wearing exactly the same outfit. Once could be luck. Now imagine it happening across the whole sky, thousands of separate patches all matching. Physicists call this the **horizon problem**. Each patch has a 'horizon': the farthest it could have heard from, set by how far light could travel in the time available."
5. "**The leading idea: cosmic inflation.** Picture a wrinkled bedsheet. Take one small square of it that happens to be perfectly smooth, then stretch it so fast and so far that it covers a whole football field. The field looks smooth everywhere, not because every part was smoothed out, but because it all came from that one smooth square. **Inflation** says something like that happened to space itself, in the first tiny fraction of a second. The part of the universe we can see began as one tiny patch, small enough to have evened out, and was then stretched to fill the whole sky."
6. "**How sure are we?** Inflation is a current theory, not a settled fact. NASA's pages say we do not know what triggered it, and that scientists are still looking for signs of it, for example in that same glow."
7. "**Why it is here.** This page runs the expansion backward at one steady speed, which is a simplification. The puzzle is a reminder that the real early universe held surprises that a steady-speed run cannot show."

*Claims to check for this section:* (a) the glow, "baby picture", the 380,000 years and "slight variations" come from NASA's baby-picture page, read in part (first 39,354 of 47,688 characters); (b) inflation's status, unknown trigger and the search for evidence come from NASA's Cosmic History page (read in full) and the baby-picture page; (c) the two cups of tea, the two strangers and "thousands of separate patches" are my own wording and an estimate with no source (no plain official page for the horizon problem was found); (d) the old paragraph's phrase "back to the Hubble time" was dropped because it mixed this page's steady-speed model with the real history; the new text ties the puzzle to the real early universe, 380,000 years in; (e) the bedsheet picture is kept from the approved page.

**A daily-life picture.** "This is the same reasoning as noticing that a candle has been burning down at a steady rate and asking: at that rate, how long ago was it full length? You do not have to have watched it burn the whole time: today's length and the burning rate are enough to work backward. This experiment does the same for each galaxy's distance and speed."

**Your job.** "Guess first, two things. Then press Run and watch three galaxies slide back toward Earth as a clock counts the years. Then add a galaxy of your own and try again."

## 2. Words and formulas (collapsed box above Part 1)
- **Big Bang:** "The name for the moment this page's backward run points to. NASA's scientists describe it as the expansion of the universe beginning about 13.8 billion years ago (see the links). The name is misleading: it was not an explosion in one place."
- **Hubble time:** "The time back to zero distance, if a galaxy had always moved at its present speed. It is the same kind of number as 1 divided by the Hubble constant."
- **Singularity:** as in the introduction.
- **Recession speed, megaparsec, billion years, constant speed:** as above.
- **Hubble's Law and the Hubble constant:** "From Cosmology 1: a galaxy's recession speed is the Hubble constant times its distance, v = H0 × d (v the speed, H0 the Hubble constant, d the distance). H0 is 70 km/s per Mpc here." *(This restates the rule the learner already met; it does not give this page's answer.)*
- **Inflation:** "A very fast stretching of space in the first tiny fraction of a second, which many cosmologists think happened. It is a current theory, not settled (NASA)."
- **Horizon problem:** "The puzzle that the sky looks almost the same temperature in every direction although distant parts seem never to have been close enough to mix. Inflation is one proposed answer." *(Wording is from the approved page; no plain official page found, see gaps.)*
- **Formula, with a worked example from the page's own numbers (held until after the first Run, §12):** "time back = distance ÷ speed. For the Virgo Cluster: 16.5 Mpc is 16.5 × 3.0857 × 10¹⁹ km = 5.09 × 10²⁰ km. Its speed is 70 × 16.5 = 1,155 km/s. 5.09 × 10²⁰ ÷ 1,155 = 4.408 × 10¹⁷ seconds, which is 13.97 billion years (one year is about 31.56 million seconds). A Mpc is 3.0857 × 10¹⁹ km." (Arithmetic checked against the module by hand.)
