## 1. Introduction (above Part 1; no answers given, §12)

**Title:** Cosmology 10: The Fate of the Universe: Does the Expansion Ever Stop?

**The question.** "Since Cosmology 1 and 2 you have known that the universe is expanding: faraway galaxies are moving apart. In Cosmology 5 you met dark energy, which changes how that expansion behaves, and in Cosmology 6 you used it to work out the universe's age. All of that looked at the past and the present. So what happens next? Does the expansion ever stop? Does it slow down, or turn around, or does it go on forever?" *(The old sentence "Experiments 5 and 6 showed that dark energy has been speeding that expansion up" is removed: it points at one answer to guess 1.)*

**What happens** *(revised 2026-11-16 at the owner's request: now about the fate of the universe; the guesses and Run material moved to its own paragraph).* "Every earlier experiment in this chapter looked at the universe as it was, or as it is today: how it began, how fast it is stretching, what it is made of, how old it is. This one looks the other way, at what happens *next*. That is a bigger question than it first sounds. In the model you will use, the universe is 13.5 billion years old today, and the graph you will see runs 100 billion years into the future: more than seven times as long as everything that has happened since the Big Bang. So we are asking about a future far longer than the whole past."

"Nobody can watch that future. What astronomers do instead is what a weather forecaster does: take what is known today and the rules that have worked so far, and run them forward. A forecast is only as good as its ingredients, and here there are just a few: how fast space is stretching today, how much of the universe's energy is matter (whose gravity pulls everything together), and how much is dark energy (which works in a different way, as you saw in Cosmology 5). Run the same rules forward with different amounts of each, and you get different futures. This experiment lets you see how different they can be."

"It is also a real test, not just a story. A model that gets the past right ought to say something sensible about what comes next, and where it cannot, because the answer depends on something nobody knows yet, that shows what is still missing. One of the model's key assumptions is being tested by new measurements right now (see 'As of November 2026' below), so the answer you will see is what the model gives, not a settled fact about the real future."

**Your guesses** *(the moved text).* "First you will look at the real universe. You will make two guesses about its future. Then you press **Run**, and the experiment takes the simple model you used in Cosmology 5 and 6 and runs it forward in time, from today to 100 billion years from now. Two pictures draw themselves: a **graph** of how big the universe becomes compared with today, and four **bars** showing how long the universe takes to double in size, once, twice, three times and four times. Your guesses are drawn on them in white. Then, in **Part 2**, you choose how much of the universe's energy is dark energy, and watch your own universe grow in the same two pictures, plus a drawing of galaxies spreading apart. You can try as many choices as you like and compare."

**Your guesses and the real pictures.** "Your guesses never change the real pictures or tables. They are drawn on the real graph and the real bars in **white**, in a box labelled YOUR GUESS, after the run, and shown again beside the results. The dark energy share you choose in Part 2 does change Pictures 3 to 5 and the pink column of the results table, but never the real pictures in Part 1. If you change a guess, press Run and see nothing change, that is not a fault: a guess is only a guess."

**Step 1: the size of the universe.** "The universe's **size** here means the distances between faraway galaxies, compared with what they are today (as in Cosmology 6). Today is 1×, and 2× means every such distance has doubled. For example, two galaxies that are 100 million light-years apart today would be 200 million light-years apart when the universe is 2× its size today. It is not the size of any one object: your own body, the Earth and our galaxy do not grow."

**Step 2: the doubling time.** "The **doubling time** is how long the universe takes to double in size. If it doubles in 10 billion years, then in 10 billion years every distance between faraway galaxies will be twice what it is today, and 10 billion years after that, four times. Comparing doubling times is a fair way to compare growth, because doubling from 1× to 2× and doubling from 100× to 200× are the same kind of step, even though the second adds far more distance."

**Step 3: the expansion rate.** "The **expansion rate** is how fast distances are growing, for each unit of distance. It is the number from Hubble's Law (Cosmology 1): at 70 kilometers per second per **megaparsec** (a megaparsec is about 3.3 million light-years), a galaxy one megaparsec away moves away from us at 70 kilometers per second, and one 100 megaparsecs away at 70 × 100 = 7,000 kilometers per second. The word 'constant' in 'Hubble constant' means the same everywhere in space at one moment. It does not mean the same for all time: in this experiment the expansion rate can change as the universe ages."

**Step 4: what sets the rate.** "What sets the expansion rate is what the universe contains. The matter and the dark energy each add a part to it. We call each one's part its **contribution**. The two contributions add up to 1 today. For the real universe, matter's is 0.30 and dark energy's is 0.70 (rounded; Planck's 2018 measurement of the matter share is 0.315 ± 0.007). You will see how the two contributions change as the universe grows."

**Step 5: what 'flat' means here.** "When we say the universe is **flat**, we mean the shape of space itself, averaged over very large distances. In flat space, the geometry you learned at school works: two straight lines that start out parallel stay parallel forever, and the angles of a triangle add up to 180°. Space could instead be curved on the largest scales, like the sphere in Curved Spacetime where two travelers who set out side by side met at the pole. This is a different idea from the curved spacetime around a star, which bends the paths of things passing close by. 'Flat' here is only about the overall shape of space once you zoom out so far that galaxies blur into a smooth background. A comparison: the Earth is curved, but a tabletop is flat for everyday purposes, because you are looking at a tiny piece of it."

"Measurements of the oldest light (Cosmology 3), combined with other measurements, are consistent with a flat universe: Planck's 2018 results give the curvature's contribution as 0.001 ± 0.002 (with BAO, a map of galaxies used with it). This experiment assumes space is exactly flat. That has a simple consequence: the contributions of matter and dark energy add up to exactly 1, with no third contribution from curvature."

**Step 6: why ask this at all.** "So far, this chapter has described the universe as it was, or as it is. This is the first time we use the model to ask where the universe is heading. That is also a test of how well we understand it: a model that gets the past right should be able to say what comes next, and the places where it cannot, because the answer depends on something we do not know, show what is still missing. You will see that the future depends on one thing above all: how much dark energy there is."

**Your job.** "First, make two guesses about the real universe. Then press Run. Afterwards, in Part 2, choose a dark energy share of your own and compare."

**What to look for.** "**Look at:** the bars, from one doubling to the next, and the gold line against the blue dashed line. **What it means:** the bars show how long each doubling takes, and the blue dashed line is the same universe with no dark energy at all. **Why it matters:** how the bars change (or do not) is the whole question. In Part 2, look at what happens to the bars and the line when you change the dark energy."

**How this page is laid out.** "Below this introduction the page has four parts. **Part 1** is where you guess and watch the real universe. **Part 2** is where you try your own dark energy share. **Part 3** shows the results and what they mean. **Part 4** is the tutor. Parts 2, 3 and 4 appear when your first run has finished. Below Part 4 comes the chapter's closing section, 'Putting It All Together'."

**What we assume.**
- "The same simple universe as Cosmology 5, 6, 8 and 9: 'flat' (explained above), containing matter and dark energy, with an illustrative Hubble constant of 70 kilometers per second per megaparsec, with the same real caveat: published values differ by how they are measured (about 70 to 76 from space telescopes and about 67 to 68 from the oldest light, NASA 2025), the 'Hubble tension'. Light and other very fast particles are left out. They mattered only in the very early universe, so they make no practical difference to the future."
- "**The dark energy is assumed to be a constant that never changes.** That is the simplest possibility, and many measurements fit it. But it is being tested: in 2025 results from the DESI survey, combined with the oldest light, favoured a dark energy that changes over time, and in 2026 other results were mixed (see 'As of November 2026' below). Nobody yet knows what dark energy is, and if it changed with time, the future could be very different. This is the biggest uncertainty in this experiment."
- "The universe is assumed to be exactly flat, as in the earlier experiments, and not worked out here. A universe that was not flat would add a third contribution to the expansion, and could behave differently in the far future. This experiment does not cover that."
- "Only the expansion is modeled. The experiment says how the size of the universe changes. It does not say anything about stars running out of fuel, black holes, or the universe becoming cold and dark, though many people think of those as 'the end of the universe.'"
- "Things held together by their own gravity do not expand: a galaxy, our Milky Way and its neighbors, the Solar System, you. Only the space between such groups stretches. This is stated here, not modeled. *(No plain official page was found that says it, so the page gives it as the model's own assumption.)*"
- "Every universe you try is matched to the real one today: the same expansion rate and the same size. A universe with no dark energy would then be younger today (9.3 billion years) than one with dark energy (13.5 billion years), as in Cosmology 6."

**Some new words:** size (of the universe), doubling time, expansion rate, contribution, megaparsec, flat, Hubble tension. All are in the Words and formulas box below.

**One more thing.** "This is the last experiment in this chapter. Once you have reached the tutor's explanation, scroll down to 'Putting It All Together'. It steps back and shows how all ten experiments fit into one story, and which parts of that story are still open."
