## 1. Introduction: "What happens" (above Part 1, no answers given)

**Title:** Cosmology 3: The Cosmic Microwave Background

**The question** (unchanged). "What is the oldest light we can still see, and what can it tell us about the early universe?"

**What happens.** *(replaces the present single paragraph; seven short parts, each with a bold lead-in, as the other introductions do)*

**1. A universe full of light, but no way to see far.** "Very soon after the Big Bang, the whole universe was filled with matter and with light, packed close together. The matter was not yet in the form of **atoms**. An atom is a tiny building block of matter. The simplest atom, hydrogen, is one **proton** (a very small particle with a positive electric charge) with one **electron** (a much lighter particle with a negative charge) held next to it. In the early universe the electrons and protons were not held together. They moved about freely, like people running around a huge, crowded room with nobody holding hands."

**2. Why light could not get far.** "Light is made of tiny packets called **photons**. When a photon meets a free electron, it gets knocked off in a new direction, a bit like a ball in a pinball machine bouncing off a bumper. In the early universe there were free electrons everywhere, so a photon could only travel a very short way before being knocked sideways, then again, and again. This is like a car's headlights in thick fog. The fog is made of tiny drops of water, and the light bounces off the drops in all directions, so the beam does not reach far and you cannot make out anything in the distance. The early universe was like being inside a fog, but a fog made of free electrons. No light could cross it in a straight line."

**3. The fog lifts.** "About {380,000} years after the Big Bang, conditions had changed enough for electrons and protons to join into atoms. An electron that is held inside an atom no longer knocks light about in the same way, so the fog cleared almost everywhere at about the same time. Light could finally travel in straight lines, and it has done so ever since. That moment is called **recombination**. (The 're-' in the name is a historical quirk: electrons and protons had never been joined before. The name stuck.)" **[recheck: the "quirk" sentence, and "almost everywhere at about the same time"]**

**4. How long ago, and how long it is.** "380,000 years sounds like a very long time, and it is against a human life. But against the age of the universe, about 13.8 billion years, it is tiny: about 1 part in 36,000. Imagine the whole history of the universe squeezed into one 80-year human life. On that scale, recombination happened about **19 hours** after birth, on the first day of life. *(calculated: 80 years × 380,000 ÷ 13.8 billion = 0.0022 year = about 19 hours)* **[recheck: the 13.8 billion years is read from NASA in Cosmology 2's audit but must be re-read for this page; the 80-year picture is a daily-life comparison for the owner to approve, §15]**

**5. Why it is the oldest light we can see.** "When you look at a faraway thing, you are seeing light that left it a while ago. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach us, so you always see the Sun as it was about 8 minutes ago. Light from the next nearest star takes about 4 years. Light from a distant galaxy can take millions or billions of years. So looking far away is looking back in time. Before recombination, no light could cross the universe, so the **first light that could ever travel to us is the light released at recombination**. Light that set off then, toward where we are now, has been travelling for almost the whole 13.8 billion years since, and is arriving now. There is nothing older that we can see with light." **[recheck: the 8 minutes and the 4 years (both well known; read a source); "nothing older ... with light"]**

**6. A "background".** "This light reaches us from all around, from every part of the sky. It is called a **background** because it lies behind everything else we can see. The stars and galaxies formed long after recombination, so they are in front of this light, the way furniture is in front of the wall of a room." **[note: "from every part of the sky" leans slightly toward guess 2 (about being the same in every direction); it says only that light arrives from everywhere, not how even it is. Owner to say whether it stays.]**

**7. Stretched on the way, so now invisible.** "Light is a wave, and the distance from one crest of the wave to the next is its **wavelength**. For visible light, the wavelength decides the colour: red light has a longer wavelength than blue. Space has been **expanding** all this time (Cosmology 1 and 2), and when space stretches, the waves in it stretch too. Draw a wave on a balloon with a marker, then blow the balloon up: the crests move apart. Light released at recombination has been stretched by the expanding universe for about 13.8 billion years. It has been stretched so much that it is no longer visible light. It is now **microwaves**: waves our eyes cannot see, in the same family as radio waves, visible light and X-rays, which differ only in wavelength. That is why we call it the **Cosmic Microwave Background**, or **CMB**. You need a special microwave detector to see it."

**In one sentence.** "The CMB is light left over from the moment the universe became see-through, travelling ever since, and now stretched into invisible microwaves."

*(Unchanged below this point, as approved on 2026-10-06 and kept by the default for question 4: "A daily-life picture" (fog on a morning drive: now partly covered by 2 above, **the owner to say whether to keep both or merge them**), "Where this shows up in everyday life", "Why it matters".)*

## 2. Part 3: "More about the CMB" (shown after the Run; teal; a panel below the check-up)

*(Placement is the owner's call: this panel, or the tutor's explanation. The wording is drafted so it can go in either.)*

**What "the temperature of the CMB" means.** "Hot things glow, and the colour of the glow depends on how hot they are: a stove element glows dull red, then orange, then white-hot. In the same way, the CMB has a glow with a **temperature**: it is the temperature of a glowing object that would give off light of this kind. We measure it in kelvin (K). 0 K is the coldest possible; room temperature is about 293 K. Today the CMB's temperature is **2.725 K**, which is about **−270.4 °C** (calculated), colder than anything you have ever touched, and only a little above the coldest possible. **[recheck: the source and the mission that measured 2.725 K; the page says "Planck satellite" at present, which may be wrong]**"

**How hot it was when it was released.** "At recombination the same light had a temperature of about **2,972 K** (calculated: 2.725 × 1,090.8). That is about **1,091 times** hotter than today. *(Then the rule and the worked sum, as the Results page now has them.)* **[sourced: ESA, "it had cooled to around 3000 Kelvin (approximately 2700ºC)". The light-bulb-filament comparison is dropped: no source fetched.]**"

**Why a hotter light is a stretched light.** *(to the tutor, not here: the mechanism is the tutor's, §12 and §13)*

**How many light particles.** "Even though it is cold and faint, it is everywhere. Every cubic centimetre of space (about the size of a sugar cube) holds roughly **400** photons of the CMB. You are being passed through by these photons all the time." **[sourced: ESA, "there are roughly 400 in every cubic centimetre of space"; the 410 I calculated is not printed. Question 7 closed: keep "roughly 400".]**

**How even it is.** "The CMB is very nearly the same temperature in every direction. The differences between one direction and another are only about **1 part in 100,000**. For 2.725 K that is about **27 millionths of a kelvin** (calculated). To get a feeling for how small that is: if the water in a swimming pool were this even, with a temperature of 293 K, the water at one end would differ from the water at the other end by only about 0.003 degrees. *(calculated: 293 K × 1 ÷ 100,000 = 0.0029 K)* The tiny differences are real and have been mapped by satellites. What they mean is a later topic." **[recheck: the 1-in-100,000 figure; the swimming pool comparison is for the owner to approve, §15]**

**How we know.** "The CMB was found by accident in 1965 by two radio engineers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, as a faint hiss in a large antenna that they could not get rid of. Later satellites measured it with great care: COBE, WMAP and Planck." *(already on the present page, kept as written)*

**What this panel does not say (held for the tutor):** why the early light was hotter; why the sky is so even (the horizon problem); what the tiny differences mean; and the Sun's own motion making one side of the sky slightly warmer than the other (1 part in 810, taught in Evidence and Imagination 7; question 8, tutor only).
