## 1. Introduction (changes only; the four "What we assume" bullets stay, with two cross-references named)

**1a-i. "What a gravitational wave is, in short."**
"In the last experiment, "Ripples in Spacetime", you saw that when something very heavy moves violently, the shape of spacetime around it changes, and that change travels outward at the speed of light as a ripple called a gravitational wave. When the ripple passes, it stretches and squeezes the space it goes through, so two detector arms set at a right angle change length a tiny amount. There the wave came at one steady strength and speed. Here the wave comes from something more specific: a pair of very heavy objects circling each other."

**1a-ii. "Two heavy objects spiralling together."**
"Some of the strongest gravitational waves come from two black holes. A black hole is an object so dense that its gravity lets nothing, not even light, escape from it. Mass is how much matter an object has, and the more mass it has, the stronger its gravity. Two black holes can be locked in orbit around each other. An orbit is a path where one object keeps going around another, like the Moon going around the Earth. As the pair circle, they send out gravitational waves, and those waves carry energy away. Losing energy makes the two slowly draw closer together, so their path is a spiral: a path that goes round and round while the circle gets smaller, like the groove on a vinyl record or the shell of a snail. This closing-in is called an inspiral. It ends when the two objects meet and merge into one."

**1a-iii. "A real example."**
"The first gravitational wave ever detected reached the LIGO detectors in the United States on 14 September 2015. It came from two black holes that spiralled together and merged about 1.3 billion light-years away (a light-year is the distance light travels in one year). The part of the signal that LIGO recorded from the end of their inspiral lasted only two-tenths of a second."

**1a-iv. "What a chirp is."**
"Scientists call the wave from an inspiral a "chirp". The name comes from sound: LIGO can turn the signals from its arms into an audible sound, and that sound is called a chirp. Scientists use the same word for the wave itself. This page does not play any sound. What the wave is like as the pair draws closer, and how it changes if it does, is what you will predict and then watch in the arms."

(Sources, fetched 2026-11-06 and listed in "Want to know more?": LIGO Lab "Sources and Types of Gravitational Waves" for the inspiral, the energy carried away, the chirp as a sound and the two-tenths of a second; NASA Space Place "What Is a Black Hole?" for the definition; the 1.3 billion light-years is LIGO Lab's figure from "What are Gravitational Waves?" as checked on 2026-11-04. "Orbit" and "spiral" are defined in everyday words with no source page; see "Source gaps". **Left out on purpose, for the tutor and Part 3:** that a closer pair orbits faster, that the pitch and loudness rise, and the 35 to 250 hertz sweep.)

**1b. "A daily-life picture"** (the coin gives both answers away, so it is replaced). New heading **"What pitch and loudness mean here."**
"A gravitational wave is not a sound, but we can talk about it the way we talk about sound. Pluck a guitar string. How fast the string shakes decides the note: a slow shake is a low note and a fast shake is a high note. That is the pitch. How far the string swings decides how loud it is: a small swing is quiet and a big swing is loud. For the detector arms below, pitch means how fast they stretch and squeeze, and loudness means how far."

**What this table tells us** (notes under each table; each item: what it is, what it means with real numbers, why it matters).
- *Table 1, frequency:* "the number of swings the arms make in one time unit, at the start and at the end of the run. It goes from 1.000 to 3.162: more than three times as high. A daily-life comparison: on a piano that is about one and two-thirds octaves higher (each octave doubles the pitch, so two octaves would be four times). It matters because this rising note is what gives the chirp its name, and it is the thing a detector uses to identify the source." (Do not give the mechanism: for the tutor.)
- *Table 1, amplitude:* "how far the arms swing, in units of strain. It goes from 0.009 to 0.028, again 3.16 times as large. Because each arm moves by half the strain, at the top of a swing the arms move by about 0.0045 from rest at the start and about 0.014 at the final moment. It matters because a louder wave at the end is easier to detect: the end of a chirp is the loudest part."
- *Table 2, shortest and longest:* "the lowest and highest length each arm reached during the run: Arm X from 0.990 to 1.012 and Arm Y from 0.988 to 1.010. They are mirror images, so one arm is as much above 1.000 as the other is below it at any moment. They are not exactly ±0.014 because the wave is still growing and the last full peak comes just before the final moment (at time 2.9, not 3.0). It matters because this is how far a detector's arms really moved." 
- *Table 2, length at the final moment:* "Arm X is 0.995 and Arm Y is 1.005 when the run stops. That is not the extreme, because the run ends 0.56 of the way through a swing, not at a peak. It matters because it shows the arms are not 'done' at the end: the wave was still going when we stopped the run."
- *Table 3:* "the light pair's final moment comes at 3.000 time units, and the heavier pair's at 0.300, ten times sooner (the time is 0.9 divided by the mass). The heavier pair is also louder: its amplitude at the final moment is 0.285 against 0.028, ten times as much. It matters because the mass sets both how long the whole chirp lasts and how loud it is overall, while the rise within a run is 3.16 times for both." (The reason for the formulas' form: tutor.)
- *Strain, plainly:* "the arms move by half the strain, so when the wave's amplitude is 0.028, the arms move by 0.014 at the top of a swing, or 1.4 percent of their rest length."
- *How sure the numbers are:* "These come from a simplified stand-in formula, not the real equations. The real signal's rise (see "A real chirp") is steeper."

**"A real chirp"** (old text kept, with sources): "GW150914, the first gravitational wave LIGO detected (2015), sweeps upwards in frequency from 35 hertz to 250 hertz (hertz means swings per second), about 7 times higher, in a signal about two-tenths of a second long, as the two black holes spiral together and merge. That is the same kind of rising "whoop" this experiment models in miniature: here the rise is 3.16 times, a gentler rise. The merger itself isn't shown here, and the mass-to-chirp relationship above is a simplified stand-in for the real physics, not the actual equations that govern a real inspiral."
