## 1. Introduction (above Part 1)
**1a. "The question"** (old: "...This is exactly the problem astronomers faced with GW170817 (Experiment 4)..."; kept, with the Experiment 4 link made explicit and nothing answered): "A single detector can tell you that a gravitational wave arrived. Can it tell you which direction the wave came from? This is the problem astronomers faced with GW170817 (the neutron-star collision in "Comparing Two Real Events"): knowing a signal had arrived was not enough. Working out roughly where it came from is what let telescopes around the world point in the right direction and catch the matching light."

**1b. "The everyday version of this idea"** (old: "...The friend closest to the shout hears it first. The other two hear it a little later..."; that sentence gives the principle behind Prediction 1, §12). New: "Imagine three friends standing far apart from each other in a field. Someone far away shouts, and each friend hears it. None of them can tell, from hearing it alone, which way the shout came from. But the three friends can compare notes: not what each heard, but **when** each heard it. The answer to which direction the shout came from is hidden in that comparison. Finding a direction this way, from the moments a signal reaches several places, is called **triangulation**."

**1c. "What happens"** (old: "...so it cannot reach every detector at the same instant unless it happens to arrive from one very special direction. It reaches whichever detector is closest to where it came from slightly sooner...", which answers both predictions). New: "Three real detectors, **Hanford** and **Livingston** (the two LIGO detectors, in the United States) and **Virgo** (in Italy), play the role of the three friends. They sit far apart on Earth (Hanford and Livingston, for example, are about 3,000 kilometres apart). A gravitational wave travels at a fixed speed, the speed of light, about 300,000 kilometres every second (written **c**). You will pick a wave that comes from one direction and watch it reach the three detectors."

**1d. The worked example "A quick worked example" is removed from the introduction.** (Old: "3,000 ÷ 300,000 = 0.01 seconds, 10 milliseconds ... ## 9. "Want to know more?" (above Part 1, visible from the start; each page fetched on 2026-11-07 by a helper, who returned summaries; I have not read the pages myself)
Plain pages (each opens in a new tab):
1. **LIGO Lab, Caltech, "Hearing the Wave"** (https://www.ligo.caltech.edu/video/ligo20171016v5): a video that compares finding a source to turning your head towards a sound, with the two LIGO detectors as your two ears; the Hanford and Livingston data are analysed together to get the direction. Beginner-simple.
2. **LIGO Lab, Caltech, "LIGO's Dual Detectors"** (https://www.ligo.caltech.edu/page/ligo-detectors): the two LIGO sites are 3,002 kilometres apart, and because gravitational waves travel at light speed the longest possible delay between them is about 10 milliseconds. Virgo is in Italy. Fairly simple.
3. **LIGO Scientific Collaboration, "GW170814: A three-detector observation of gravitational waves from a binary black hole coalescence"** (http://ligo.org/science-summaries/GW170814/): the first wave seen by all three detectors. It reached Livingston first, Hanford 8 milliseconds later and Virgo 6 milliseconds after that; adding Virgo cut the possible area of sky from 1,160 square degrees (two detectors) to 100, and to 60 after the final analysis. Virgo is at Cascina, near Pisa, Italy. Semi-technical, readable.
4. **LIGO Scientific Collaboration, "GW170817"** (https://ligo.org/detections/gw170817/): before Virgo joined, the possible region of sky "wrapped around" the sky; once it joined, the region became far smaller, and telescopes then found the galaxy NGC 4993 and the light about 11 hours after the wave. Beginner-friendly.
5. **LIGO Lab, Caltech, "GWTC-5.0: Updated LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA Catalog sets new records in precision gravitational wave astronomy"** (https://www.ligo.caltech.edu/news/ligo20260526, 26 May 2026): two detectors place a source on a ring on the sky, and a third detector such as Virgo sharpens that by triangulation; the record, GW240615, was placed within about 6 square degrees. News style, readable.

6. **"GW170817: Observation of Gravitational Waves from a Binary Neutron Star Inspiral"** (https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.05832): the abstract gives the sky region, 28 square degrees, and the distance, about 40 megaparsecs. Technical.
7. **"Multi-messenger Observations of a Binary Neutron Star Merger"** (https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.05833): the abstract gives the first sky region, 31 square degrees. Technical.
