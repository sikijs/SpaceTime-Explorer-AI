## 1. Introduction (above Part 1)
**1a. "The question"** (kept, wording tightened; asks, does not answer, so it stays): "The last three experiments, "The Chirp", "Where the Energy Comes From" and "Comparing Two Real Events", built a toy model of two objects spiralling together: a wave whose pitch and loudness build as they circle. None of them asked what happens if the pair itself is moving relative to the detector. If the source of a gravitational wave is racing away from us as it sends the wave out, does that change what we detect?"

**1b. "The Doppler effect"** (kept; an ambulance example the learner knows; the new part is the number): "You already know this effect from everyday life, even if you have never named it: an ambulance's siren sounds higher-pitched as it comes towards you, then suddenly drops to a lower pitch once it has passed and is driving away. Nothing about the siren changed. What changed is that each new sound wave starts from slightly farther away than the last one, so the waves reach your ear a little more spread out. This is called the **Doppler effect**, and it applies to any kind of wave, such as sound, light or a gravitational wave, whenever its source is moving relative to whoever is detecting it. The word for a wave that arrives stretched to a lower pitch than it was sent at is **redshift** (first defined in the Gravity and Curved Spacetime chapter). The Doppler effect is one cause of redshift: motion."

**1c. New paragraph, "How much stretch? An everyday example":** "Here is a way to put a number on it. Imagine a lamp that flashes once every second and is moving away from you. Light travels at a speed called **c**, so a speed of 0.5c is half the speed of light. Between one flash and the next, the lamp moves 0.5c × 1 second farther away, so the second flash has a little farther to go than the first. Each flash arrives 1.5 seconds after the one before it, not 1 second. The flashes are stretched to 1.5 times as far apart. In general the everyday stretch is 1 plus the speed measured in c, so at 0.5c it is 1 + 0.5 = 1.5. A bigger stretch means a lower pitch. This is the amount that an everyday Doppler effect alone predicts, and it is the amount this experiment compares against."

**1d. "What happens"** (old: "...The detector's arms will pulse at the detected frequency — slower than the frequency the source actually emitted", which gives the answer to Prediction 1, §12). New: "You will see the same chirp as in the last three experiments, now sent by a source that is moving away from the detector. The picture shows the detector's arms, and beside them two waves: one drawn as the source sent it, labelled **Emitted**, and one drawn as the detector receives it, labelled **Detected**. A second panel under them puts numbers on how much each wave is stretched."

**1e. "Your job"** (old: "...predict whether the detected frequency will be higher, lower, or the same..."; kept in meaning): "In Part 1, make two predictions. First, will the detected frequency be higher, lower or the same as the frequency at the source? Then, will the real stretch match the everyday Doppler stretch described above, or be more or less than that? Then press Run once and watch. In Part 2, choose a speed of your own and see how the stretch changes. Then the tutor will ask you what you saw."

**1f. New paragraph, "Your predictions and the real pictures"** (CLAUDE.md §14, rule 7): "Your predictions never change the real pictures, the tables or the numbers: the arms, the waves, the bars and the tables come from a calculation that knows nothing about what you guessed. Your guesses are drawn on the picture in white, in boxes labelled YOUR GUESS, and listed again beside the results. In Part 2, the speed slider does change the drawings; the marks it moves are pink."

**1g. New paragraph, "A word before you run: Doppler factor":** "The picture and the numbers use a **Doppler factor**: the number you multiply the pitch the source sent by to get the pitch the detector receives. A factor of 1 means no change, and a factor less than 1 means the detector hears a lower pitch. The **stretch** is 1 ÷ the factor: it says how many times longer each wave is when it arrives than when it was sent. A stretch of 2 means each wave is twice as long."

**1h. "What to look for" paragraph removed from the introduction** (it said "Detected" would fall behind, stretch out and shrink and that the gap is "slightly bigger than an everyday Doppler effect alone", both answers; first report C7). Its content moves into the caption, where each item appears only once its feature is drawn.

**1i. "What we assume"** (kept, with the earlier chapters named, and "approaching case" worded): "Everything "The Chirp" and the experiments after it assumed still applies: the effect is hugely exaggerated, only one wave pattern is shown, no real detection hardware is modelled, the merger itself is not modelled, and the chirp formulas are simplified stand-ins, not the real equations." Then three bullets kept: the source moves directly away from the detector only, with no sideways motion and no approaching case; this experiment models only the motion-based part of redshift, and for a real, very distant source the larger part of its redshift is usually the expansion of space while the wave travels (not modelled here; see Part 3); the source's mass is fixed so that the experiment stays on one idea. The speeds up to 0.9c are a toy: no real binary of this kind is known to move this fast; the page says so.

**1j. "Some new words":** "The "Words and formulas" box below has each one, with a short plain meaning: Doppler effect, redshift, c, Doppler factor, stretch, everyday stretch, emitted, detected, receding, chirp, pitch, strain, cycles behind, time dilation, cosmological redshift."

## 9. "Want to know more?" (above Part 1, visible from the start; each page fetched on 2026-11-07 by a helper, who returned summaries; I have not read the pages myself)
Plain pages (each opens in a new tab):
1. **NASA Goddard, Imagine the Universe, "Doppler Shift"** (https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/features/yba/M31_velocity/spectrum/doppler_more.html): the Doppler effect for sound and light, a fire truck's pitch rising and falling, and that light from a source moving away is shifted to a lower frequency and a longer wavelength, called redshift. It says the simple formulas hold only well below light speed. Beginner-simple.
2. **NASA Goddard, Imagine the Universe, "The Farthest Visible Reaches of Space"** (https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/features/cosmic/farthest_info.html): what redshift is (light shifted to redder, longer wavelengths) and that astronomers use it to judge how far away distant galaxies are. Beginner-simple. It describes redshift as motion, so it is linked for "redshift", not for the cosmological kind.
3. **NASA Science, "Hubble Cosmological Redshift"** (https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/science-behind-the-discoveries/hubble-cosmological-redshift/): that the expansion of the universe stretches light to redder wavelengths, and that this is different from redshift caused by an object moving away through space. Beginner-simple.
4. **NASA Science, "What Is Cosmological Redshift?"** (https://science.nasa.gov/asset/webb/what-is-cosmological-redshift/): a short Webb infographic: stars and galaxies keep their size while the space between them grows. Beginner-simple.
5. **LIGO Scientific Collaboration, "Improving measurements of the cosmic expansion with gravitational waves"** (http://ligo.org/science-summaries/O3Cosmology/): that the masses of black holes measured from a wave are redshifted by the expansion of the universe, so they look larger than they are, "just like the light from a receding galaxy" is stretched. It does not mention GW150914. Beginner to intermediate.
Original paper (opens in a new tab):
6. **"Observation of Gravitational Waves from a Binary Black Hole Merger"** (https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.03837): the abstract gives GW150914's distance, about 410 megaparsecs, "corresponding to a redshift z = 0.09". Technical; linked only for that figure.
Not linked: LIGO's "Latest constraints on cosmic expansion and general relativity with gravitational waves" (https://ligo.org/science-summaries/o4b_cosmology/), which is weaker for a beginner, and a candidate page for GW150914's detector-frame masses, which was not found on an official plain page (§29 rule 3(c): the page never claims it).
