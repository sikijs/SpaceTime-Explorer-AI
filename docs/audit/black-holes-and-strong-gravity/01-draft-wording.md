## 1. Introduction (above Part 1; gives no answers, §12)

**Title:** Black Holes 1: How Small Must Something Be to Become a Black Hole?

**The question.** "In Gravity and Curved Spacetime, Experiment 7, you saw that when a mass is packed tightly enough, even light cannot escape it, and that is a black hole. That experiment used made-up amounts. Here we use real objects and real numbers, and ask: how small would a real object, such as the Earth or the Sun, have to be squeezed to become a black hole?" *(kept from the approved text)*

**Sub-heading: What is a black hole?** "A black hole is a region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing that gets close enough can ever leave it, not even light. Its edge is called the **event horizon**, or just the **horizon**. NASA describes it as a boundary that contains all the matter that makes up the black hole, not as a surface like the Earth's." *(NASA, "Black Holes", updated 12 Aug 2026; to be re-checked.)* "The horizon is not a wall. It is a one-way boundary: anything can cross it going in, and nothing, not even light, comes back out. Because no light comes out, we cannot see a black hole itself. We see what it does to things nearby."
[Image 1: M87*, the EHT image, already in the project. Caption: "The first picture of a black hole's shadow (April 2019). The bright ring is hot gas just outside the horizon; the dark middle is the black hole's shadow. It is a real observation, not a drawing." Credit: "EHT Collaboration (CC BY 4.0), via ESO."]

**Sub-heading: A black hole is not a vacuum cleaner.** *(kept, shortened)* "Far away, a black hole pulls exactly like any other object with the same mass. If the Sun were replaced by a black hole with the Sun's mass, the Earth would keep orbiting just as it does now. A black hole is special only very close to it, and this experiment is about how close 'very close' is." [Own drawing 2: Earth on the same orbit around the Sun, then around a small black dot of the same mass, labelled "same pull at this distance", "illustration, not to scale".]

**Sub-heading: How stars become black holes.** *(kept, with the size of the star)* "A star keeps its size because two things balance: gravity pulls everything inward, and the heat and pressure from the nuclear reactions in the core push outward, like air inside a balloon. When a star with more than about eight times the Sun's mass runs out of fuel, NASA says, its core collapses and the star explodes as a supernova. What is left depends on the core: a white dwarf, a neutron star, or, for the heaviest cores, nothing stops the collapse and the core becomes a black hole." *(NASA, "Black Hole Types", updated 12 Aug 2026; to be re-checked.)* [Image 3: Hubble's Crab Nebula, the remains of a star that exploded in the year 1054. Credit: "NASA, ESA and Allison Loll/Jeff Hester (Arizona State University). Acknowledgement: Davide De Martin (ESA/Hubble)." Caption: "A real observation of a supernova remnant: the glowing gas thrown out when a massive star exploded. It shows what is left of a star's outer layers." — *corrected 2026-11-16 after the live page was read: Hubble's page calls it a supernova remnant 6,500 light-years away in Taurus and says nothing about its core or the year 1054, so neither is printed.*] [Own drawing 4: the life-stage strip: massive star, supernova, then neutron star or black hole by core mass, labelled "illustration".]

**Sub-heading: How we know they are there.** "Two main ways. First, **stars circle them**. At the centre of our galaxy, a star called S2 sweeps around a point where we can see nothing; its orbit needs the pull of about four million Suns packed into a tiny space. Two teams tracked such stars for decades and shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics. Second, **gas glows**. Gas falling toward a black hole speeds up and heats until it shines brightly, just outside the horizon. Both the gas and the stars are outside the horizon; the black hole itself gives off no light." *(ESO eso2017, 6 Oct 2020, and eso2208 read; "about four million" is the figure on the ESO page, see Question A.)* [Image 5: S2's orbit (ESO, a real VLT observation from 2002; credit "ESO"). Caption: "A real observation. Left: the crowded centre of the Milky Way with S2 marked. Right: the path S2 followed from 1992 to 2002. This is an early picture; later measurements followed S2 through its closest approach in 2018." Image 6: Sgr A*, the EHT image already in the project; credit "EHT Collaboration (CC BY 4.0), via ESO." Caption adds "12 May 2022; the black hole at the heart of our galaxy, 27,000 light-years away".]
[Image 7: Webb's picture of the galaxy M77 (28 May 2026): "A real observation. Black holes millions of times heavier than the Sun sit at the centres of most large galaxies; the glowing core here is gas near one. The black hole itself cannot be seen." Credit "ESA/Webb, NASA & CSA, A. Leroy". *The helper's source for "about 8 million Suns" was a search snippet; the draft says only "millions of times", unless the image page is read.*]
[Image 8: Gaia BH3, an artist's impression (ESO). Credit "ESO/L. Calçada/Space Engine (spaceengine.org)". Caption: "An artist's impression, not a photograph. A giant star orbits a dark black hole 33 times as heavy as the Sun, found in 2024 only because its pull makes the star wobble." *(ESO eso2408; "33 solar masses", "next most massive known in our galaxy, Cygnus X-1, 21".)*]
