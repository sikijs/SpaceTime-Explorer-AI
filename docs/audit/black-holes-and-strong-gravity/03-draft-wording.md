**A naming problem in S3 (for the owner).** S3 says "6 times the black hole's gravitational radius, sometimes called the Schwarzschild radius". On this project's usage, the horizon (Schwarzschild) radius is 2GM/c², and 6 times GM/c² is **3 horizon radii**. The page's "gravitational radius" means GM/c². The draft therefore writes "6GM/c², which is 3 horizon radii" and never repeats S3's "sometimes called the Schwarzschild radius". The link description says what S3 says, in the page's own terms ("6 times the black hole's gravitational radius"), and the Words box explains the difference.
**Gaps (§29 rule 3(c)):** no plain official page was found that says a merging pair of black holes plunge together once they reach the last safe orbit (a LIGO page and a search returned only papers); the Gravitational Waves chapter covers mergers, so the draft names it and does not claim it. No official page was found that gives the model's two-orbit "rosette" amount for a particular number of turns; the draft gives no precession number.

## 2. Introduction
**The question.** *(kept)* "In Gravity and Curved Spacetime, Experiment 6, you saw that a planet stays in orbit because it moves sideways as it falls, so it keeps missing the thing it falls toward, and that this works at any distance. Close to a black hole, gravity is as strong as it ever gets (you saw that in Black Holes 2). So is it still safe to circle a black hole, at any distance outside its edge? Or is there a place where an orbit cannot last?"

**Why even ask this.** *(kept)* the paragraph about planets and GPS satellites keeping steady orbits, and the assumption that "if you can get into an orbit, you can stay there".

**Going round in a circle (new).** "Think of swinging a stone on a string. Spin it too slowly and it drops. Spin it too fast and the string is pulled from your hand. At one speed in between, the stone goes round in a perfect circle. Gravity is the string for a planet. In Gravity 6 you saw that this works for any distance: pick the distance, then give the planet the right sideways speed, and it circles. Real orbits are never perfectly clean, though. A grain of dust, a passing star or a gentle push can nudge an orbit a little. So the question is not only 'can a planet circle here?' but 'if a circle gets a small nudge, does it stay a circle?'" *(No answer is given. The marble comparison is held back until after the first Run.)*

**What happens.** *(kept, with two changes marked)* the six steps, with step 2 now reading "Two small probes (think of tiny spacecraft) are placed at the same distance, one in each picture" and step 6 now reading "Pictures show each probe's path, how far each is from the black hole, and, for a second, far-out start, the same two paths at 20 horizon radii."

**Some words we will use.** *(kept: circular orbit, nudge, plunge, horizon, horizon radius, astronomical unit; and the probes' speeds as a fraction of the speed of light.)* New: "**The last safe orbit** is the closest distance at which a circular orbit can still survive a small nudge. Closer in than that, a nudged circle does not last." *(The value is not given here.)*

**The black holes.** *(kept)* the three black holes of Black Holes 1 and 2.

**Real orbits we have seen, and recent news (as of November 2026).**
- **A star whose orbit does not close (ESO, 16 April 2020).** "The star S2 circles Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the centre of our galaxy, once every 16 years. At its closest it is less than 20 billion km from the black hole, which is 120 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, and it moves at almost 3% of the speed of light. ESO's telescope followed it for about 27 years and found that its orbit is shaped like a rosette and not like an ellipse: each lap is turned a little compared with the one before, as Einstein's theory predicts. The same turning was first seen in the orbit of the planet Mercury." *(S1)*
- **The fastest known star (ESO, 19 August 2026).** "In August 2026 astronomers announced a star named S301. It goes round Sagittarius A* in just 8.7 years and reaches speeds of 25,000 km a second, over 8% of the speed of light. At its closest it is 1.78 billion km from the black hole, about 12 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun: closer than any other star observed so far. Its next closest approach is in 2031. The team hopes to use it to measure how fast Sagittarius A* spins." *(S2; the paper was not read)*
- **How far out is that?** "Measured the way this page measures, in horizon radii: S2's closest approach is less than about 1,600 horizon radii from Sagittarius A* and S301's is about 140. Both stars are far outside the distances this experiment is about. They show that Einstein's theory shapes real orbits even far out; they do not show what happens at the last safe orbit." *(computed here: see section 9, to be confirmed against ESO's own four-million-Suns figure, which gives about 150 for S301)*
- **The gas around a black hole (NASA).** "Gas that falls toward a black hole forms a disk. NASA's HEASARC describes the inner edge of that disk as limited by a minimum distance within which material cannot orbit. NASA's NICER team reported on 30 May 2024 that models of the disk which used to stop at that edge now follow the gas right down to the horizon. Neither page is something this experiment shows: they are given only to say where the idea turns up." *(S3 and S4; both held back from the introduction's links until after the first Run, because they give the size of the edge)*

**Pictures.** The S2 orbit image (ESO, labelled as an early observation) and the EHT picture of Sagittarius A* (EHT Collaboration, via ESO), each credited as for Black Holes 1; one own labelled drawing, **"Two rules for a circle"**: left, a ring of orbits at every distance, each labelled "ordinary gravity: a circle at any distance"; right, the same rings with the innermost ones drawn dashed and labelled "?", with no value given. *(The drawing is a plan; it shows the question, not the answer.)*
