## 2. Introduction
**The question.** *(kept)* "In Experiment 2 of this chapter you compared two clocks held at rest, one far from a black hole and one hovering close to its horizon, and saw that the hovering clock ticks differently. That experiment said nothing about a traveler who falls in. So what happens to a traveler who falls straight toward a black hole? What does their own watch show, what does a clock far away show, and what do they feel on the way?"

**Why even ask this.** *(kept, with the last clause changed)* "Picture yourself as the traveler. You let go, and a black hole waits below you. There are no engines and no rope, nothing to hold you back, and once you cross the horizon there is no way back. A friend stays far away, watching over the whole thing with a clock of their own, while you carry a watch on your wrist. In everyday life this would not be a puzzle. Drop a stone and it reaches the ground in a moment, and everyone watching agrees how long it took. We take for granted that all clocks agree on how long something lasts. But in Experiment 2, two clocks near a black hole did not agree about how fast they tick. So it is fair to ask whether you and your friend would tell the same story of your fall: how long it lasts, and what you feel on the way down." *(removed: "with the pull on you growing stronger at every step".)*

**Why the feet are pulled harder than the head (new, with drawing 2 and a real example).** "Gravity pulls harder on whatever is nearer to the thing doing the pulling. You can see this with the Moon: NASA explains that the Moon's pull is strongest on the points of Earth closest to the Moon and weakest on the points farthest away, and that uneven pull is part of what raises the tides. Now picture a traveler falling feet first toward a black hole. Their feet are slightly nearer than their head, so the feet are pulled slightly harder, and the traveler is stretched. We measure the stretch as the difference in pull between the two ends of a 2-meter body, in **g**: 1 g is the pull of Earth's gravity at its surface. It is the same kind of effect as the tidal effect you met in Gravity and Curved Spacetime, Experiment 3." *Drawing 2 (made for the page, schematic, not to scale): a body falling feet first toward a black hole at the bottom, with a longer arrow at the feet and a shorter arrow at the head, labelled "pulled harder (nearer)" and "pulled less (farther)". No black-hole numbers, no size comparison between black holes.*
