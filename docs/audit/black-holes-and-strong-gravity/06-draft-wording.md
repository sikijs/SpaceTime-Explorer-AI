## 2. Introduction
**The question.** *(kept, one clause changed)* "In Experiment 5 you followed a traveler falling into a black hole and compared two clocks: the traveler's watch and a clock at rest far away. That was a comparison of clocks. It was not what a far-away observer would actually *receive*, because light takes time to travel, and Experiment 5 left that out. This time we put it back. What does an observer far away actually receive from the falling traveler, and do they ever see the traveler cross the horizon?"

**Why even ask this.** *(kept)* "Picture yourself as the observer, far from the black hole, watching a friend fall in. ... So ask yourself: when your friend reaches the horizon, do you see it happen? And if you do, when?"

**How light climbs out (new, with Drawing 2).** "Light always takes time to get from one place to another. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach us (from the kept 'light travel time' sentence). Near a black hole there is a second thing to keep in mind, and the pictures will show it: the journey to the observer is not the same from every starting point." *Second paragraph (rewritten 2026-11-18 for clarity, approved by the owner):* "Think of a deep pit with a ladder up the side. Someone standing at the top has almost no climbing to do. Someone at the bottom has the whole depth to climb before they reach the top. Gravity around a black hole works a little like that pit. The closer to the horizon a flash starts, the deeper in it is, and the more of a 'climb' it has to make to get out to an observer far away. So a flash sent from close to the horizon has a longer journey than a flash sent from far out. This is only a picture to help you think about it. The experiment will not use the pit, only the real numbers." *(Gives no number and no verdict.)* **Drawing 2: "Two flashes."** "A schematic drawn for this page, not to scale, with no numbers. A flash sent from far out and a flash sent from close to the horizon, each on its way to the observer. The second arrow is drawn longer, because it has further to climb." *Look at:* the two arrows. *Meaning:* the flash from nearer the horizon has more climbing to do. *Why it matters:* the observer will receive the two flashes at different times, even if they were sent at the same instant on the same watch.
