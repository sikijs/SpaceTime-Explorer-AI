## 1. Introduction

**1a. Heading (CHANGED).** "What You See Is the Past: Where Is the Spaceship, and Where Does It Look to Be?" (the "Experiment 4 —" prefix goes, as on the other audited pages).

**1b. "The question" (CHANGED; the postcard opening goes).**
"When you look at something through a telescope, you do not see it as it is now. Light takes time to travel, so the light that reaches your eye left the thing a while ago. You already know this for the Sun: it is about 8 light-minutes from Earth, so its light takes about 8 minutes to reach us and we see the Sun as it was 8 minutes ago. The nearest star system, Alpha Centauri, is about 4.3 light-years away, so we see it as it was about 4.3 years ago. Now think of a spaceship that is *moving*. While its light is on the way to you, the spaceship keeps flying. So the place where it *looks* to be is not the place where it *is*. This experiment asks: where does the spaceship look to be, and does its image move at the same speed as the spaceship itself? The first three experiments of this group were about what is **measured**: the times and distances an observer works out from their own ruler and synchronized clocks, after allowing for the time light takes to travel. Experiment 3 ended by saying that this is not what the observer sees with their own eyes. This experiment is about what is **seen**."
*(The 8 minutes and the 4.3 years are from NASA's "Cosmic Distances" page, read 2026-11-21: section 9. The page gives Alpha Centauri, not Proxima Centauri, so the draft says "star system".)*

**1c. NEW, "A light trip takes time" (Figure A).**
"Look at Figure A. A spaceship is 3 light-seconds from the lab. It sends out a flash of light, and the light needs 3 seconds to cross those 3 light-seconds. When the flash arrives, the telescope shows the spaceship where it *was*, 3 seconds ago, and not where it is now. A daily-life comparison: the light of a star you see tonight left the star years ago, and the star may have changed since."

**1e. NEW, "The map of space and time" (Figure B).**
"**The map of space and time.** Figure A showed one flash on a single line. To follow a spaceship that keeps moving, we need to show *where* it is and *when* it is there, both at once. A map can do that. Think of a train timetable: it tells you that the train is at one station at 9:00 and at the next station at 9:30. The map of space and time is that kind of timetable, drawn as a picture. **Figure B, just below, is such a map. Read the steps here with Figure B in front of you.**
1. **The two edges.** The bottom edge is *where*: distance from the lab in light-seconds (0, 1, 2, 3, 4). The left edge is *when*: time on the lab's clock in seconds (0, 1, 2, 3, 4), with later times higher up. One dot on the map is one event: one place at one time.
2. **The lab: the sky-blue dashed line.** The lab stays at position 0 all the time. As the seconds pass it only goes up, so its line is straight up and down. A thing that stays still always draws a straight up-and-down line.
3. **The spaceship: the orange line.** It starts near the bottom, about 1 light-second from the lab, and ends near the top, about 3 light-seconds from the lab. It goes up (time passes) and also to the *right* (farther from the lab). So in Figure B this example spaceship is flying *away* from the lab. (Figure A's spaceship flew toward the lab. Either direction works the same way. A line leaning toward the right means moving away from the lab, and a line leaning toward the left means moving toward it.)
4. **How light is drawn: the two yellow lines.** Figure B has no separate picture of a flash. The two yellow lines *are* the paths of light, as you will see in the next step. Light covers 1 light-second every second, so on the map, for every 1 step to the side, light goes 1 step up (1 light-second across takes 1 second). Steps of equal size to the side and up make a line slanting at exactly 45 degrees, like the diagonal of a square, and that is the slant of both yellow lines. (The ship's orange line leans less than 45 degrees away from straight up, about 28 degrees. That is right: a spaceship is slower than light. A line leaning *more* than 45 degrees would mean covering more than 1 light-second in 1 second, which is faster than light, and nothing can do that.)
5. **The moment of looking: the yellow dot and the past light cone.** The dot is on the lab's line, at about 3.2 seconds. The light that reaches the telescope at this moment left earlier, so it came from lower on the map, and it came from both sides. So think of two flashes arriving at the dot, one from the left and one from the right. Follow each one backward in time: that gives the two yellow lines going *down* and outward from the dot at 45 degrees. Together they are the **past light cone**. Every event on a yellow line sent out light that arrives at the telescope exactly now. So the telescope shows you the events on the yellow lines.
6. **The two squares: what you see, and what is true.** The orange line has two squares on it. Read them one at a time.
   - **The hollow square, "Where the spaceship looks to be".** It is where the orange line crosses the right-hand yellow line. Look straight down from it to the bottom edge: it reads about **1.7**, so the spaceship was 1.7 light-seconds from the lab. Look straight left from it to the left edge: it reads about **1.5**, so this was at 1.5 seconds on the lab's clock. At that time the spaceship sent out the light that the telescope is receiving right now.
   - **The journey of that light.** Follow the yellow line from the hollow square up to the yellow dot. It goes 1.7 across and 1.7 up, so the light took about 1.7 seconds. It left at 1.5 seconds, and 1.5 + 1.7 = 3.2, which is the moment of looking. The light is old: it left 1.7 seconds before the telescope looks.
   - **The solid square, "Where the spaceship really is now".** To find it, start at the yellow dot (3.2 seconds) and go straight across, sideways, to the orange line. Everything on that level is at the same moment, 3.2 seconds. Now look straight down from the solid square to the bottom edge: it reads about **2.6**, so the spaceship is really 2.6 light-seconds from the lab at this moment.

   **Putting them together.** The telescope shows the spaceship at 1.7 light-seconds, but it is really at 2.6. The two are about 0.9 light-seconds apart, because the spaceship kept flying away during the 1.7 seconds the light was on its way. The gap between the squares is the gap between what you see and where the spaceship is.

The path in Figure B is only an example, drawn to show the idea. Its speed is not the speed of any run on this page, and the numbers above are read off the picture, so they are approximate."

## 8. Physics additions (tests first, §9, §10)
1. `PART_ONE_DIRECTION = 'approaching'`, `PART_ONE_SPEED = 0.6` constants.
2. `closedFormImageSpeed(direction, speed)` = v ÷ (1 − v) or v ÷ (1 + v). Existing tests already check these forms; the function is new for the check-up.
3. `guessStretch(prediction, speed)` returns the stretch of the speed strip a guess names: slower = [0, v], same = the tick at v, faster = [v, strip end]; `STRIP_MAX_C = 2`; with tests that the real image speed (1.5c) lies inside the "faster" stretch for Part 1, that each guess gives a different stretch, and that every stretch is inside the strip.
