"A clock moves past the lab at a steady speed. It ticks once every second of its own face, as the moving clock did in the first chapter. Two of its ticks are our two events: tick 0, and tick 1 one second later by the clock's own face. Three observers will each measure the same two events. The first is the lab, which sees the clock move. The second is the clock itself, for which the clock is simply at rest. The third is a passing observer who moves past the lab. The prediction is about a number you can make from any observer's two readings, using one fixed recipe: square the time, square the distance, then subtract the second from the first. For example, an observer who reads a time of 5 seconds and a distance of 3 light-seconds gets 5 × 5 = 25, then 3 × 3 = 9, then 25 − 9 = 16. You will not calculate it yourself. You only predict how the lab's number compares with the clock's own: bigger in the lab, the same in both, or smaller in the lab. Then run it and look at the numbers all three observers find. In Part 2 you choose both speeds yourself."

**1d. New paragraphs, "Space and time as one thing" (owner-approved 2026-11-19; three paragraphs).**
"In everyday life we keep space and time apart. Space is where something is, measured in meters or kilometers. Time is when it happens, measured in seconds or hours. We use two different rulers, one for each. Yet to pin down an event you need both. "Meet me at the station" is not enough. You also need "at 3 o'clock". A ball landing, a clock ticking and a flash of light each have a place and a moment."
"In 1908 the mathematician Hermann Minkowski told a conference: "Henceforth, the old conception of space for itself, and time for itself shall reduce to a mere shadow, and some sort of union of the two will be found consistent with facts." In plain words, he was saying that space on its own and time on its own are only shadows. A shadow shows only part of an object, and its shape changes as the light moves. In the same way, the time between two events and the distance between them each change depending on who is measuring. What is real, he said, is some kind of combination of the two. Minkowski gave this talk three years after Einstein's first paper on relativity. He had been Einstein's mathematics teacher, and he was the first to see that Einstein's results could be described as a single "space and time" taken together, now called spacetime."
"This experiment looks at one small piece of that idea. It does not try to explain all of spacetime. It asks only this: for the same two events, can we build one number from the time between them and the distance between them, so that we can ask whether different observers find the same number or different numbers? You will make the prediction, see the real numbers, and then the tutor will help you work out what they mean."

## 3. "Words and formulas" (collapsed box)

- **Event:** something that happens at one place at one moment: a clock ticking, a flash going off, a ball landing.
- **Observer:** someone, or something, that measures events from their own position and motion.
- **The lab:** the observer for whom the lab itself stands still. The clock moves past the lab.
- **At rest:** not moving compared with the observer you are talking about.
- **Light-second:** the distance light travels in one second, exactly 299,792,458 meters (about 300,000 kilometers). Measuring distance this way puts it in the same units as time.
- **c:** the speed of light. 0.6c is six-tenths of that speed.
- **Time between:** how many seconds an observer finds between two events.
- **Distance between:** how many light-seconds an observer finds between two events, measured in that observer's own frame.
- **time² − distance²:** multiply the time by itself, multiply the distance by itself, subtract the second from the first.
- **Interval squared:** the name of that number. (Shown only after the Part 1 run, because the name belongs to the tutor, §12.)
- **Interval:** the square root of the interval squared. (Shown only after the Part 1 run.)
- **Formula (shown only after the Part 1 run):** time² − distance². Worked example from this page, for the lab at 0.6c: the time between the ticks is 1.25 s and the distance is 0.75 ls, so 1.25 × 1.25 − 0.75 × 0.75 = 1.5625 − 0.5625 = 1.00. For the clock itself: 1.00 × 1.00 − 0.00 × 0.00 = 1.00.

**Caption, "How to read the results picture":**
*What you see.*
- **Three rows**, one for each observer, in the same order as the table below. Each row shows what that observer found for the same two ticks.
- **Blue bar (time):** the time that observer found between the ticks, in seconds ("s"). A longer bar means more time.
- **Pink bar (distance):** the distance that observer found between the ticks, in light-seconds ("ls"; one light-second is the distance light travels in one second). No pink bar means the observer found both ticks at the same place. The blue and pink bars share one scale, set by the longest bar.
- **Green block (time² − distance²):** the number you predicted about, worked out from that row's own two numbers. The blocks are all the same length on purpose: their length is not the size of the number. Read the number written in the block.
- **The side scale:** the same three numbers on a scale of their own, from 0 to 2, so that they can be compared by position. The lime dots are the real values.
- **The white mark and the YOUR GUESS box:** your guess, placed beside the real values. It is only a marker. It never moves the real values.

*A worked moment.* At 0.6c the lab finds 1.25 s between the ticks and 0.75 ls. So 1.25 × 1.25 − 0.75 × 0.75 = 1.5625 − 0.5625 = 1.00. The clock's own frame finds 1.00 s and 0.00 ls: 1.00 × 1.00 − 0 × 0 = 1.00.

*What to look for.* (Each item appears only once what it refers to is drawn.)
1. **Look at** the blue and pink bars of the lab row and of the clock's own row. **What it means:** the lab finds more time (1.25 s against 1.00 s) and some distance (0.75 ls against none). **Why it matters:** the two observers disagree about both numbers, which is the starting point of the question.
2. **Look at** the three lime dots on the side scale and the numbers beside them. **What it means:** the number time² − distance² for each observer, 1.00 in every row. **Why it matters:** it puts the number you were asked about side by side for all three observers, so you can see whether the observers disagree about it.
3. **Look at** the white YOUR GUESS mark and the lab row's lime dot. **What it means:** where your guess sits against the real number for the lab. **Why it matters:** it sets what you expected beside what was found. (Which of the two explanations is right is left to the tutor.)

| Observer | What we would expect | What was found | A plain reading |

| The lab | 1.00 (the clock's own face reads 1 second between its ticks, so its squared interval is 1.00) | 1.00 | the same |

(Numbers from the module and tested; shown to two decimals. The reading is "the same" or "different", with no invented cut-off.)

**What this table tells us.**
- **What it is:** the three observers' time and distance between the same two ticks, and the number worked out from each pair.
- **What it means with the real numbers:** the lab finds 1.25 s and 0.75 ls, the clock finds 1.00 s and 0.00 ls, and the passing observer finds 1.01 s and 0.14 ls. Those differ. But 1.25² − 0.75² = 1.00, 1.00² − 0² = 1.00, and 1.01² − 0.14² ≈ 1.00 (rounding).
- **Why it matters:** it shows that the three observers disagree about time and about distance but agree on one number made from the two. Why they agree, and whether it holds for other pairs of events, is left to the tutor and to later experiments.
- **How sure the number is:** the three results come from a tested calculation (the Lorentz transformation of "Moving frames"). No instrument measured them. The 1.01 and 0.14 are rounded; the exact figure is 1.00.

## 10. Physics module (new, with tests; the approved model is unchanged)

In `spacetimeInterval.ts`:
- `intervalScaleEnd(result)` returns 2 (the fixed end of the side scale), and
- `guessMarkerOnScale(result, guess)` returns, for `'bigger' | 'same' | 'smaller'`, the marker position on the 0 to 2 scale (1.5, 1.0 or 0.5 times the clock-frame row's value, as a fraction 0 to 1 for drawing) together with the clock-frame row's real value and the lab row's real value. It refuses an unknown guess.
- `checkUpRows(result)` returns for each observer the expected value (the clock's own face time squared, from the clock-frame row), the found value, and `'the same'` or `'different'` (equal within 1e-9; no invented cut-off).
- `runIntervalExperiment` is unchanged; Part 1 calls it with 0.6 and 0.5.

Tests (in `spacetimeInterval.test.ts`):
1. The scale ends at 2, and a real value of 1.00 sits at 0.5 of the scale.
2. The marker for "same" lands exactly on the clock-frame row's value; "bigger" is above it and "smaller" below it, at 0.5 of the value on each side.
3. The marker positions are 0.75, 0.5 and 0.25 as fractions of the scale for "bigger", "same" and "smaller".
4. The real result at 0.6c and 0.5c is the same for every guess (1.25 s, 0.75 ls; 1.00; 1.00; 1.00).
5. `checkUpRows` returns the clock's own face time squared as the expected value for each observer, and "the same" for all three, at 0c, 0.6c and 0.99c and several passing speeds.
6. An unknown guess is refused.
7. Existing tests are untouched and still pass.
