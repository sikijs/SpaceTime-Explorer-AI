## 1. Introduction

### 1a. "The question" (CHANGED; replaces both paragraphs, explains the owner's sentence in detail, no answer given, §12)

> **The question.** In Experiment 5 we found that when several particles are taken together, the whole set has a mass of its own, the system's mass. You work it out from the total energy and total momentum, and every observer agrees on it. We also saw that energy of motion can change form: when two lumps of clay hit and stick, their energy of motion became internal energy, and that made the lump heavier.
>
> Particle physicists use this idea on purpose. They smash particles together to make a particle that was not there before, and the mass of the new particle has to come from the energy of the collision. This section explains what that sentence means, and then asks a practical question about it.

### 1b. "Making a new particle: what it means" (NEW; the detailed explanation, with a picture; every claim is used before the run only as a definition, nothing compares the setups)

> **A particle that was not there before.** We do not mean a particle that was hiding inside the others and got shaken loose. We mean one that did not exist until the collision. Before it there were, say, two particles. Afterward there are those two and a third. Think of a clap. The sound was not hiding inside your hands, waiting to get out. It did not exist until your hands met, and it was made from the energy of their motion. A new particle is made in a similar way: it did not exist before the collision, and it is made from the energy of motion of the particles that collided. (The comparison only goes so far, because a particle is a piece of matter with a mass, and a sound is not. The next point explains how energy can become something with a mass.) *(Changed 2026-11-22 at the owner's request: the freezer comparison was dropped because ice is water in another form, so the water was there before; it was the opposite of a particle that was not there before.)*
>
> **What rest energy is.** Every particle, even one sitting perfectly still, has energy just because it exists. That energy is stored in its mass, which is what Experiment 5's rule *E = mc²* says: *E* is the energy, *m* is the mass, and *c²* is a fixed, enormous number (the speed of light multiplied by itself). This stored energy is called the *rest energy*, because it is the energy a particle has when it is *at rest*. Think of a battery in a drawer. It is not doing anything, but it has energy stored inside it. A particle's rest energy is a store like that, except that the store is its mass itself. A particle that is moving has more energy than that: its rest energy *plus* its energy of motion (the next point explains where that extra comes from). To feel how big c² is, take a paper clip of about 1 gram. Its rest energy is about 90 trillion joules, enough to keep a 100-watt light bulb shining for about 28,000 years (worked out here from E = mc²; that energy is locked inside the clip and cannot simply be got out). For a single electron the rest energy is 0.511 MeV, and for a proton it is about 938 MeV (an MeV is a million electron-volts, a tiny unit of energy used for particles).
>
> **How motion adds energy, and where K comes from.** Energy never appears from nowhere. It is always moved from somewhere else. To make something move, you have to push it, and the push hands it energy. Take a shopping cart. Standing still, it has only the energy stored in it. Push it and it rolls: it now has *energy of motion* as well, and that energy came from you, from the food your muscles burned. The faster you make it go, the more energy of motion it has, and the more you had to give. A particle is the same, except that the push is far bigger. In a real machine, electric forces push the particles again and again along their path, each push adding a little more energy of motion, and the energy comes from the electricity that runs the machine. So a particle's total energy is its rest energy plus the energy of motion it was given: *total energy = rest energy + energy of motion*. In our unit (explained next), a particle at rest has an energy of 1. Give it an energy of motion of 3, and its total energy is 1 + 3 = 4.
> **Where does K come from?** K is the energy of motion that has been given to the particles that are about to collide, added up for all of them. In the real world it comes from the machine that pushed them. In this experiment you decide it: K is a setting, like a dial. It is the money in your wallet for the price tag below, and a bigger K means a bigger machine.
>
> **The machine: how an accelerator gives particles their energy of motion.** An accelerator is a machine that pushes particles faster and faster, until they carry a lot of energy of motion. CERN's accelerators, which feed the Large Hadron Collider, show how the parts fit together. Everything below about CERN's machines is from CERN's own pages; the comparisons to a swing and to a battery are ours.
> 1. **A source of particles.** For protons, the source is a bottle of hydrogen gas. A hydrogen atom is one proton with one electron, and an electric field strips the electrons off, leaving the protons. CERN says one bottle lasts for months: it is replaced only twice a year.
> 2. **A pipe with almost nothing in it.** The particles fly inside a metal pipe from which nearly all the air has been pumped out. CERN says the vacuum is as empty as interstellar space. If the pipe held air, the particles would keep crashing into air molecules and lose their energy.
> 3. **Cavities that push.** Along the way there are metal chambers called radio-frequency cavities. Inside each one an electric field pushes the particles forward. Each time a group of particles passes, some of the energy of the radio waves in the cavity is handed to them. The field flips between "forward" and "backward" very fast (400 million times a second in the Large Hadron Collider), and it is timed so that the particles always arrive when it points forward. It is like pushing a child on a swing: a push at the right moment adds energy, and a push at the wrong moment would take some away. Each push is small. CERN says the Large Hadron Collider's cavities add up to at most 16 million volts per lap for a beam. A particle with the charge of a proton gains 1 electron-volt for each volt it crosses, so that is at most about 16 MeV per lap (worked out here from CERN's figure). The final energy is 6.5 million MeV. So the particles go round and round: 11,245 laps every second, and more than 10 million passes through the cavities in about 20 minutes.
> 4. **Magnets that steer.** With nothing to steer them, the particles would fly off in a straight line. Dipole magnets bend their path so that they curve round the ring, and the more energy the particles have, the stronger the magnets must be. Quadrupole magnets work like lenses: they squeeze the beam into a narrow stream. At the Large Hadron Collider the beam is squeezed from 0.2 millimeters to 16 micrometers just before the collision, so the particles are far more likely to hit each other. A magnet changes which way the particles go, but it does not speed them up. That is the cavities' job. CERN's magnets are superconducting: their coils are cooled to 1.9 kelvin (−271.3 °C), so cold that electricity flows through them with no resistance. CERN says that with ordinary magnets the ring would have to be about 120 kilometers around instead of 27.
> 5. **A chain of machines, in two layouts.** One machine cannot do everything. CERN joins accelerators in a chain, each handing its beam on to the next, bigger one, and the Large Hadron Collider is fed by a chain of four. The beam enters it at 450 GeV, and the cavities raise it to 6.5 TeV, which CERN says is more than 14 times as much. The layouts are these. In a *linear* accelerator the beam goes from one end to the other, with a single pass of pushes, so more energy means a longer machine. In a *circular* accelerator the same particles go round and round and get a boost at every lap, and the limit is how strong the magnets are. Last, the particles are brought together. In a collider, two beams travel in opposite directions and meet head-on. In the other arrangement, one beam is fired at a target that is sitting still. Those are the two setups this section compares.
>
> All of those pushes, added up, are what we call K, the energy of motion supplied. The machine's job is to pay in K. The collision's job is to spend it.
>

>
> **The principles behind the machine.** (1) *An electric field pushes on electric charge.* Gravity pulls on a ball and makes it roll down a slope; an electric field pushes on a charged particle in the same way. The "height of the slope" is the voltage. A particle with the charge of a proton or an electron gains 1 electron-volt for each volt, so crossing 16 million volts gives 16 MeV. A particle with no charge feels no push, which is why accelerators use charged particles. (2) *The push has to arrive at the right time.* The field flips between forward and backward. If the particles arrived while it pointed backward they would be slowed, so they travel in bunches timed to arrive when it points forward. CERN also says that a particle a little early or a little late is sped up or slowed a little, and that this is what sorts the beam into bunches. (3) *A magnet steers a moving charge, but does not speed it up.* A magnet pushes a moving charged particle sideways, at a right angle to its motion, like the string of a ball swung in a circle: the path curves, the speed does not change. CERN says the more energy the particles carry, the stronger the magnets must be, so the higher the energy, the stronger the magnets or the larger the ring. (4) *Close to the speed of light, pushes add energy but hardly any speed.* Nothing with mass can reach the speed of light (Experiment 5). Each push still adds energy of motion, but the particles speed up less and less. CERN says only "close to the speed of light". A 6.5 TeV proton has about 6,930 times its own rest energy (worked out here from 6.5 TeV and 938 MeV), so a K of several thousand is possible in our unit even though the speed hardly changes.
>
> **How a run goes, step by step** (a machine like the Large Hadron Collider, from CERN's pages). *Fill:* protons from the bottle of hydrogen go through the chain of four smaller accelerators, which also divide them into bunches; each beam is made of about 2,500 bunches and each bunch starts with about 160 billion protons. *Injection:* the beams enter the big ring at 450 GeV, two beams in two separate pipes, in opposite directions. *Acceleration:* the cavities push the bunches round, 11,245 laps a second; it takes more than 10 million passes, about 20 minutes, to reach 6.5 TeV; the magnets have to get stronger as the energy rises. *Squeezing:* just before the beams meet, the lens magnets squeeze them to a very narrow stream (CERN compares it with firing two needles from 10 kilometers apart so that they meet halfway). *Collision:* the beams cross at four places where four large detectors record what comes out; CERN's key figures give about 1.5 billion collisions a second in ATLAS and CMS. *The end of a run:* special magnets steer the beams away to a place called the dump. *(Added 2026-11-22 at the owner's request. Principle 1, the first half of principle 3 and the 6,930 figure are textbook physics or worked out here, not lines on a CERN page; the bunch figures, the 1.5 billion and the needles are from CERN's Large Hadron Collider page; the rest is from the four CERN pages in the links table.)*
>
> **Counting in rest energies, and the price tag.** In this section we do not use joules or MeV. We use the rest energy of one colliding particle as our unit, and call it 1. It is like measuring a room in "paces" instead of meters. If your pace is 0.75 meters, a room that is 6 paces long is 4.5 meters long: only the ruler changed, and the room did not. Here is what "1" means for two real particles. If the colliding particles were electrons, then 1 would be 0.511 MeV, 2 would be 1.022 MeV and 5 would be 2.555 MeV. If they were protons, then 1 would be 938 MeV and 5 would be about 4,690 MeV. The pattern of the numbers is the same for both, which is why one unit is enough. In this unit, *a mass and an energy are the same number*, because rest energy is stored mass. One colliding particle has a rest energy of 1 and a mass of 1. A new particle of mass X, counted in masses of one colliding particle, has a rest energy of X. For example, a new particle with X = 2 is twice as heavy as a colliding particle, so its rest energy is 2. And K = 5 means that the energy of motion you supply is five times the rest energy of one colliding particle. Now the *price tag*. To make a new particle of mass X, *X rest energies of energy must be paid*. It is like paying at a till. A loaf costs 2 coins: with 1 coin in your hand you get nothing, not half a loaf, and with enough coins you get the whole loaf. In the same way, a new particle with X = 2 costs 2. If the energy you are able to pay with is less than 2, nothing new appears, and if it is 2 or more, the particle can be made. The two colliding particles are *not* paid for again: they are still there afterward, each with its own rest energy of 1. Only the new particle has to be bought. So the books look like this. Before the collision there is 2 (the two particles) plus K. Afterward there is 2 (the same two particles) plus X (the new one) plus whatever energy of motion is still left over. The payment is made once, when the particle is created, and afterward the particle carries that energy in its mass. How much of K can really be spent on the price is the question this section is about. *(Expanded 2026-11-22 at the owner's request; the paper-clip figure is computed from E = mc², not from a source; the electron and proton figures are CODATA values as in Experiment 5; "electric forces push the particles, and the energy comes from the electricity" is general physics, not taken from a source on this page.)*
>
> **Why it has to come from the collision.** Energy cannot appear from nowhere: the total is the same before and after, like money moved between bank accounts. Before the collision there are two particles (2 rest energies of mass) and some energy of motion, K. After it, the two particles are still there (2 again), and the new particle (X more). So the energy of motion is the only thing that can pay for the new particle. If it pays enough, the new particle is made. If it does not pay enough, nothing new appears and the particles just bounce on.
>
> **A comparison from daily life.** Rub your hands together and they get warm: energy of motion has become heat. Drop a clay ball and it lands with a thud: energy of motion has become a flattened ball that is slightly warmer. A collision between particles does the same kind of thing, except that, when there is enough energy, some of the energy of motion becomes *new mass* instead of heat.
>
> **Why it needs so much energy.** Because c² is huge, even a light particle costs a lot of energy. A new particle's cost is always its rest energy, and the energy has to be gathered into a very small space at one moment: CERN describes this as packing enough energy into a tiny region so that the energy can become mass. CERN's Large Hadron Collider does it with beams of protons at 6.5 TeV each (2015 to 2018; CERN gives 6.8 TeV now). In everyday terms that is tiny, about as much energy as a falling safety pin carries (CERN's comparison), but it is squeezed into a space far smaller than the head of the pin.
>
> **The practical question.** You choose how much energy of motion to give the colliding particles. That amount is K. Some of it may be able to change form into the mass of a new particle. How much? Is it all of K, or only part of it? And does it depend on how you arrange the collision? You could fire one particle at another that is sitting still, or you could fire two particles at each other. In both cases the particles are given the same K. This section lets you compare the two, and you will be asked to predict which one lets more of K become the mass of a new particle.

### 1d. "The formulas we need" (CHANGED, §12: the two-setup worked numbers leave; each formula keeps a worked example that cannot answer the prediction)

> - **The system's mass again.** A particle at rest has energy 1. If it also has an energy of motion K, its total energy is 1 + K, and its momentum × c is √((1 + K)² − 1), because for one particle E² − (pc)² is its mass squared, which is 1. For example, take a single particle with K = 3: its energy is 4, and its momentum × c is √15 = 3.873. For a whole set of particles, add up the energies and the momenta (a piece moving left counts as negative), and then M = √(E_total² − (P_total c)²). For example, a set with a total energy of 5 and a total momentum × c of 3 has M = √(25 − 9) = √16 = 4. *What it does not mean:* M is not the sum of the particles' separate masses.
> - **Usable energy:** U = M − 2. In words: "the system's mass, minus the rest masses of the two colliding particles." For example, a system with M = 4 has U = 2. *What it does not mean:* it is not the energy of motion measured in the lab.
> - **Locked-in energy:** L = K − U. In words: "what is left of the energy supplied." For example, if K = 3 and U = 2, then L = 1.
> - **The threshold:** a new particle of mass X can be made only if M ≥ 2 + X. For example, a new particle of mass X = 1 needs M ≥ 3.

Then, **moved here from the introduction**, the explanation the introduction no longer gives:
  - **Why part of the energy is locked in.** Momentum is conserved. The beam particle in Setup A brought momentum with it (5.92 in units of rest energy), so the whole system must keep moving after the collision, at 0.845c. That motion carries energy that cannot also be spent on mass. In Setup B the momenta cancel (+ and −), so the system ends at rest and nothing is kept as motion.
  - **A daily-life example.** Two cars crash. A car runs into a parked one and the wreck slides on: the wreck's motion keeps part of the energy, and only the rest crumples metal and makes heat. Two identical cars meet head-on at equal speeds and the wreck stops dead: all the energy of motion goes into the crumpling. The new particle's mass plays the part of the crumpling.
  - **Why the observer riding along sees it differently.** The system's own observer moves with the wreck, so for them it never moves, and the locked-in energy is zero: all that is left is M. That is why M is the number that matters, and why every observer agrees on it.
  - **The real world.** A machine that smashes a beam into a target at rest wastes most of the beam's energy this way; a collider avoids it. The experiment's own formula, M² = 2m² + 2mE for a beam on a target at rest, gives a system mass of about 110 GeV for a 6.5 TeV proton beam on a proton at rest, while the LHC's two head-on beams of 6.5 TeV each give 13 TeV (2015 to 2018, Run 2); CERN's page now gives 6.8 TeV per beam and 13.6 TeV. (The 110 GeV is computed here, not from a source. CERN says colliders give a higher collision energy because the energies of the two particles are added.)
