## 1. Introduction

Heading: "Cause Before Effect: Can the Order of Events Change?" (the "Experiment 3 —" prefix goes).

**1a. "The question"** (the approved paragraph, with two sentences changed: the ending now points at Figure A, and "Earlier experiments gave us a reason" is kept):
"Imagine a flash of light is sent out, and when it reaches a light-sensitive switch, a firecracker goes off. The flash is the **cause**, because it makes the bang happen. The bang is the **effect**. In everyday life, nobody would ever find that the firecracker went off before the flash was sent. Whoever you ask, the cause comes first, and an effect never comes before the thing that caused it. Earlier experiments gave us a reason to look at this more carefully. They showed that "at the same time" can depend on who is measuring: two far-apart events can be at the same moment for one observer and at different moments for another. If observers can disagree about whether two events are simultaneous, a bigger question follows. Can they also disagree about which of the two events came *first*? And if they can, does that break cause and effect? Could some observer, somewhere, find that the firecracker went off before the flash was sent? This experiment is about finding out when the order of two events can change, and when it cannot. The next few sections build up the pieces, with pictures."

**1b. NEW, "What a cause does: it makes a trip" (Figure A).**
"Look at Figure A. A cause does not reach its effect instantly. Something has to travel from one to the other, and that takes time. Here the flash is sent at 0 seconds from a lamp. It travels across the room to the switch, which takes 1 second because the switch is 1 light-second away. At 1 second it arrives, and the firecracker goes off at once. In the picture, distance is across the page and time goes up, so the trip is the slanting line, and the three events, the flash sent, the flash arriving and the bang, are three points on it, one above another in time. Everyone who watches this chain agrees on its order: sent, then arrives, then bang.
Here is the same thing in daily life. You post a letter on Monday. It reaches your friend on Wednesday. Your friend writes back, and you get the reply on Friday. The reply cannot arrive before your letter was posted, because it is an answer to it. Every link in a chain like this is a trip that takes time, and the trip is why the order of the links is fixed."

**Figure A (a drawing):** distance across (0 to 2 light-seconds), time up (0 to 2 seconds). A blue square at (0, 0) labelled "Flash sent (the cause)". A yellow slanting line to (1, 1), where a yellow dot is labelled "Flash arrives at the switch". A short upward arrow from there to an orange square just above, labelled "Firecracker goes off (the effect)". A vertical dashed line at distance 1 labelled "the switch, 1 light-second away". A note: "An illustration, not a measurement."
Caption, "How to read Figure A": name each shape; "the slanting line is the flash travelling at 1 light-second every second; a steeper-looking line would be a slower signal"; "what to look at: the chain goes upward in time, and every link needs the trip before it."

**1c. NEW, "What 'effect before cause' would look like" (Figure B).**
"Now picture the order going wrong. Imagine the firecracker going off first. The bang happens, and only afterwards the flash is sent and travels to the switch. That would be an effect with no cause behind it yet. Everyday examples of the same scrambling are an egg that is whole again before it was dropped, a reply that arrives before the question was asked, or a verdict that is read before the trial begins. Nobody has ever seen such a thing, and the whole of science depends on it never happening: if effects could come before their causes, we could not say that one thing makes another happen. Figure B puts the normal order beside the scrambled one so that you can see exactly what is meant. The scrambled version is **imagined**. It is drawn only so that we know what we are looking for, and it is not something that was measured or that this experiment shows."

**Figure B (a drawing):** two rows. Row 1, "The order we expect" in blue and orange: a time arrow with three boxes left to right, "1. Flash sent (cause)", "2. Flash arrives", "3. Bang (effect)". Row 2, "Effect before cause (imagined)", in grey-violet with a dashed border: "1. Bang", "2. Flash sent", "3. Flash arrives", with a small label "IMAGINED: not measured, not a result". 
Caption: what each row is; that the colours are the same events in a different order; that row 2 is a picture of an idea.

**1d. NEW, "Seeing is not the same as finding out" (Figure C).**
"There is one more idea to be careful with. An observer does not see events at the moment they happen, because light takes time to reach their eyes. Suppose a firecracker goes off 3 light-seconds away. Its light takes 3 seconds to arrive. So an observer who looks at that moment sees it 3 seconds after it happened. A careful observer does not simply trust what reaches their eyes. They know how far away the firecracker is, so they take away the travel time: 3 seconds after seeing it, minus 3 seconds of travel, gives the moment it really happened. When this experiment says an observer **finds** the order of two events, it means the order they work out in this careful way, from their own ruler and their own synchronized clocks. It never means the order in which the light happens to reach their eyes. (A later experiment is about what an observer *sees*.)"

**Figure C (a drawing):** a time line for the observer. A grey dot at 0 s labelled "The firecracker goes off, 3 light-seconds away". A yellow arrow along the line to 3 s labelled "The light takes 3 seconds to arrive". A white-outlined eye icon at 3 s labelled "The observer sees it now (time 3 s on their clock)". A curved arrow back to 0 labelled "Careful observer: 3 s − 3 s of travel = it happened at 0 s". 
(White is **not** used here, because white means the learner's guess: the eye icon is drawn light blue.)
Caption: what the arrow, the dot and the eye mean; the worked sum 3 − 3 = 0; units.

**1e. NEW, "Two things from the earlier experiments" (Figures D and E).**
"The last experiment drew the **light cone** of an event. Look at Figure D. It shows an event O, "here, now", at the middle of the ruler at time 0, and the two paths of a flash of light that leaves it, one going right and one going left. The paths spread out like the sides of a V. Together they are the edges of O's light cone. A signal that never goes faster than light starts at O and can reach only the places on or between those edges. That is what "could O be the cause of something?" means in this experiment: the something must lie on or between the edges.
The first chapter also drew a **same-moment line**. Look at Figure E. For the lab, which stands still, the line through O is level. It joins all the events that the lab finds happening at the same moment as O. A passing observer, who moves, has their own ruler and clocks, and finds a different set of events at the same moment as O. Their same-moment line through O is **tilted**, and the faster they move, the more it tilts. You do not need to work out why: the first chapter showed it, and this experiment uses it. Figure E shows the lab's line and the line of one passing observer."
(Not said here: which side of a line an event can be on, whether B can be above or below it, or what the cone's edges say about the tilt. Those are what the learner is asked to predict and what the tutor explains, §12.)

## 3. Words and formulas (collapsed)
Entries (short plain meanings; formulas with a worked example from the page):
- **Event**, **cause**, **effect**, **observer**, **light-second**, **c**: as in 1f.
- **Finds, sees**: "sees" is when light reaches your eyes; "finds" is what you work out after taking away the light's travel time (Figure C).
- **Light cone, edge of the cone**; **same-moment line**; **order** ("O first", "the same moment", "B first").
- **Time between**: signed; positive means B is later than O, negative means B is earlier. Formula, passing observer: time between = γ × (t − u × x), where u is the observer's speed as a fraction of c, t is the lab's time, x the lab's distance, and γ = 1 ÷ √(1 − u²). Worked: t = 3, x = 5, u = 0.3: γ = 1 ÷ √(1 − 0.09) = 1.048; t − u × x = 3 − 1.5 = 1.5; time between = 1.048 × 1.5 = 1.57 seconds.
- **Time² − distance²**: for the lab, 3² − 5² = 9 − 25 = −16; every observer finds −16.
- **Reachable, light only, unreachable** (after the run only, §12).
- **The flip speed**: the speed at which O and B are at the same moment, t ÷ x. Worked: 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6. (Shown after the run only.)
