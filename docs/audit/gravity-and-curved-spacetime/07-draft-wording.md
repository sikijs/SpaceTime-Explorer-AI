## 1. Introduction (above Part 1; no answers given, §12)

**Title:** Experiment 7: What Is a Black Hole?

**The question.** "Imagine squeezing the whole Sun into a ball a few kilometres across: smaller than a city. Squeeze it that much and gravity near it becomes ferocious. This experiment asks one simple, big question: can a lump of matter ever be squeezed so tightly that its pull becomes too strong to get away from, even at the greatest speed there is?"

**Words first (§15).**
- "**Escape speed**: the speed you must have, when you start from a place, to get away from a mass for good and never fall back. Throw a ball up gently and it comes back down. Throw it faster and it goes higher. At the escape speed it never returns. (On Earth the escape speed at the surface is about 11 kilometres a second [recheck: not yet sourced; remove if no source is found].)"
- "**The speed of light**: the fastest speed anything can have. Experiment 5 of 'Relativity of Time and Motion' showed that nothing can go faster."
- "**Mass and concentration**: 'concentrated' means how much mass is packed into a given space. A golf-ball-sized lump of lead is far more concentrated than a foam ball of the same size."

**What happens.** "You will decide how heavy a central object is. A probe will be launched straight up from the same height above it every time, so only the mass changes, and it will be launched at the speed of light, the fastest launch possible. You will watch whether it gets away or falls back."

**Your job.** "First you will guess what happens for a very light mass, a very heavy mass, and whether anything special happens somewhere in between. Then you will watch three launches, and then you can try your own masses."

**Real black holes are real (no answer given).** "Astronomers have photographed the glowing gas around two of them, using radio telescopes all over the Earth working together as one giant telescope: one at the centre of the galaxy M87 (shown in 2019) and one at the centre of our own Milky Way (shown in 2022). Near the end of this page, a 'field guide' shows both pictures. For now, notice only that this experiment is a simplified version of the reasoning that first led people to wonder whether such objects could exist."

**What we assume.**
- "This uses ordinary gravity, like Experiments 2, 3, 5 and 6 ('Gravity and Curved Spacetime'), not the exact general relativity a real black hole needs."
- "The mass, distance and speed of light in the probe model are relative, made-up amounts, not real numbers. The 'field guide' at the end uses real numbers and says so each time."
- "The probe goes straight outward, not sideways (orbits were Experiment 6)."
- "The central mass does not move."

**Your guesses never change the real pictures or tables** [Predictions rule 7]. "Your guesses are drawn in white on the real graph and shown again beside the results. They never change a picture or a table. The mass you choose in Part 2 does change the drawing, and the graph, which is why the guesses stay locked while you try other masses."

**Some new words:** escape speed, event horizon, probe, relative units, and the others in the "Words and formulas" box.

## 3. "Words and formulas" (collapsed box)

- **Escape speed:** "the speed needed to get away from a mass and never come back. Formula used here, for a mass M at distance d: escape speed = √(2 × M ÷ d). Worked example in this page's units: mass 0.8, distance 1: √(2 × 0.8 ÷ 1) = √1.6 = 1.26. The speed of light here is 2, so that is 63 percent of light speed."
- **Speed of light (c):** "the top speed in the universe. In this page's made-up units it is 2."
- **Probe:** "the little test object we launch. It stands for anything, including light."
- **Event horizon** (shown after Part 1's run): "the invisible boundary around a black hole. From inside it nothing, not even light, can get out. It is not a wall or a surface. It is a place of no return."
- **Black hole:** "an object squeezed so tightly that it has an event horizon."
- **Concentrated:** "a lot of mass in a small space."
- **Relative units:** "numbers that only compare things (this is twice that) rather than real kilometres or kilograms."
- **Schwarzschild radius** (field guide only): "the size an object would have to be squeezed to in order to become a black hole. Formula: radius = 2 × G × M ÷ c². Worked example, Earth: G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹, M = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s gives about 0.0089 m, about 9 millimetres." [computed by me; NASA's page says "about 9 mm", [recheck]]
- **Shadow, ring, accretion disk, supernova, singularity, gravitational waves, Hawking radiation:** short one-line meanings, written when the field guide is approved.

## 10. The black hole field guide (new, after the tutor, opens when the tutor's explanation appears)

Tone: playful and plain, with everyday comparisons. Each section ends with "Go deeper" links. Everything is "as of November 2026" and the dates sit beside the figures. **Real numbers are labelled "Real black holes: measured by astronomers" or "Real physics, calculated by the formula, not by the probe model".** Source facts below are as the fetched pages gave them [recheck each before print].

**F1. What black holes are NOT: myth or fact?** (a three-question mini-quiz, answers revealed on a click)
- "Do black holes suck everything in like cosmic vacuum cleaners?" NASA: "Black holes don't suck in other matter. From far enough away, their gravitational effects are just like those of other objects of the same mass." Daily-life comparison: if the Sun were swapped for a black hole of the same mass, the planets would keep orbiting as before.
- "Is the event horizon a surface you could land on?" NASA: "It's a boundary that contains all the matter that makes up the black hole."
- "Is a black hole a hole?" It is a very heavily concentrated lump of matter.
- Links: NASA, "Black holes" (science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/); NASA, "10 questions you might have about black holes".

**F2. A 240-year-old idea.** A short story: John Michell reads a paper to the Royal Society in 1783 imagining "dark stars" whose light could not escape; Laplace revived the idea in the 1790s; Schwarzschild found the exact answer in 1916, a month after Einstein's general relativity. Plain-words note: Michell used escape speed, exactly this page's reasoning. **Gap (§29 3(c)): no plain official page found.** The St Andrews MacTutor page and a German-only Max Planck page were read; see question B.

**F3. How small would it have to be? (the size ladder; real physics)** An interactive strip: pick an object, see the radius it would need to be squeezed to. Values from the formula 2GM/c² (mine; to be computed by a tested module function): the Moon about 0.11 millimetre; the Earth about 9 millimetres (NASA agrees: "about 9 mm"); Jupiter about 2.8 metres; the Sun about 3 kilometres (NASA agrees: "about 3 km"); Sagittarius A* (4 million Suns) a radius of about 12 million kilometres; M87* (6.5 billion Suns) a diameter of about 38 billion kilometres, which agrees with the ESO release's "just under 40 billion kilometers across". A person: far smaller than an atom's nucleus. Own labelled illustration: nested circles drawn to a log scale, so Earth, Sun and a city can share the page.
- Links: NASA Imagine the Universe, "Black holes" (`imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/science/objects/black_holes1.html`).

**F4. Where do black holes come from?** An illustration of a massive star's life ending: fuel runs out, core collapses, supernova. NASA: stars of "more than eight times the Sun's mass" end as stellar black holes of "a few to hundreds" of Suns. Then the sizes: stellar, intermediate ("difficult to confirm"), supermassive (hundreds of thousands to billions of Suns), and the theorised primordial ones, with no definitive evidence yet. About 50 confirmed or suspected in the Milky Way, estimates of up to 100 million.
- Links: NASA, "Types of black holes"; NASA, "Anatomy of a black hole".

**F5. Pictures of the invisible: the Event Horizon Telescope.** Embedded images with credits (subject to the owner's approval and a final look at each file): M87* (2019, ESO eso1907a, credit "EHT Collaboration", CC BY 4.0) and Sgr A* (2022, ESO eso2208-eht-mwa, credit "EHT Collaboration", CC BY 4.0). Plain explanation: radio telescopes across the Earth work together as one planet-sized telescope; the glowing ring surrounds a dark centre called the shadow; the ring's size agrees with Einstein's prediction. Facts: M87* is 6.5 billion Suns at 55 million light-years; Sgr A* is 4 million Suns at about 27,000 light-years; Sgr A* looks about as big in the sky as "a doughnut on the Moon"; the M87* ring is 42 ± 3 microarcseconds across, Sgr A*'s 51.8 ± 2.3. Newer: 2024, Sgr A* in polarized light (twisted, organised magnetic fields); 2025, M87*'s polarization pattern flipped between 2017 and 2021 ("totally unexpected", the shadow stayed the same).
- Links: ESO eso1907; ESO eso2208; ESO eso2406; EHT papers on arXiv (1906.11238 and 2311.08680); the ASIAA release (an institute release, not the EHT's own page).

**F6. Hearing black holes collide.** LIGO, 14 September 2015: two black holes of about 29 and 36 Suns merged 1.3 billion years ago; the signal reached Livingston 7 milliseconds before Hanford; the stretch detected was under one ten-thousandth of a proton's width. In 2025, GW250114 gave the best test yet of Hawking's area theorem. As of the GWTC-4.0 catalogue, 218 candidate signals in all; the fourth observing run ended 18 November 2025. Own diagram of a "chirp" (rising pitch and loudness, then a short "ringdown") drawn by us, labelled as a sketch, not data. A LIGO image (credit "Courtesy Caltech/MIT/LIGO Laboratory") only after I have seen it.
- Links: LIGO, press release GW150914; LIGO, GW250114; LIGO Scientific Collaboration, GWTC-4.0 summary; LIGO, end of the fourth run.

**F7. Our neighbours.** Gaia BH1 (about 10 Suns, about 1,560 light-years, Ophiuchus, 2022), Gaia BH2 (about 10 Suns, 3,800 light-years, Centaurus, 2023) and Gaia BH3 (33 Suns, about 2,000 light-years, Aquila, 2024), a drawn map of where they sit compared with the Sun, to scale on one line. All are in orbit with an ordinary star and were found from the star's wobble. "None of them threatens us."
- Links: ESA, "Gaia discovers a new family of black holes"; ESO, eso2408 (Gaia BH3).

**F8. The oldest and the biggest.** JWST: UHZ1, 470 million years after the Big Bang (Chandra and Webb, November 2023); GN-z11, about 430 million years after the Big Bang, with a black hole of 2 million Suns; CAPERS-LRD-z9, about 500 million years after the Big Bang, the earliest confirmed by its spectrum as of this page, up to 300 million Suns (McDonald Observatory release, a university observatory, not NASA or ESA). A 2026 Webb result is a "black hole star" cocoon at 1.8 billion years (not a record). Biggest: NASA lists TON 618 at 66 billion Suns, and Holmberg 15A at "at least 40 billion". Smallest known: 3.8 Suns. "These numbers are the ones NASA gives; they are disputed and change as astronomers measure again."
- Links: NASA/Chandra, UHZ1; NASA, GN-z11; the McDonald Observatory release; ESA/Webb, weic2610; NASA, "Black holes".

**F9. Do black holes last forever? (Hawking radiation)** NASA says black holes "slowly shrink because they are losing tiny amounts of energy called 'Hawking radiation'," and that for most this would take far longer than the age of the universe. Plain explanation kept to what NASA says; the popular "particle pairs" picture is not used, because it is considered misleading by some sources and was not verified here. **Gap (§29 3(c)): no dedicated plain official page.** The NASA question-and-answer page is the only source read.
