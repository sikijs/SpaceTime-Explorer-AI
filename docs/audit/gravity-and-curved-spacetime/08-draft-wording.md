## 1. Introduction (above Part 1; no answers given, §12)

**Title:** Experiment 8: Does Gravity Bend Light?

**The question.** (Kept as approved, with the chapter name added.) "Experiment 7, 'What is a black hole?', showed that light aimed straight at a massive enough object can't escape it. But what about light that isn't aimed at the mass at all, light that just happens to pass nearby? Does gravity do anything to it?"

**What happens.** "You will guess three things, then watch light pass a fixed mass three times. Each time, you will see how closely the light passes, its 'aim': how close it would come if gravity did nothing at all. A graph fills in as you watch. Then you can choose an aim of your own."

**Your job.** "Guess first. Then watch what the light does, and what the graph shows. Then try your own aim and compare."

**Your guesses and the real pictures.** "Your guesses never change the real pictures or the numbers. They are drawn on the pictures in white, in a box marked YOUR GUESS, and shown again beside the results. The aim you choose in Part 2 does change the drawing and the graph, because it is a real setting. Your guesses do not."

**Words first (§15).**
- "**Aim distance** (the aim): how far from the mass the light's straight path would pass if gravity did nothing. Aim 2 means two units from the mass."
- "**Ordinary gravity**: the pull between masses you met in Experiments 1 to 7, the same pull that makes a ball fall. This page's animation uses it."
- "**Relative units**: the mass, the distances and the speed of light on this page are made-up amounts, so only comparisons matter. The speed of light here is 2."
- "**The speed of light**: the fastest speed anything can have. Experiment 5 of 'Relativity of Time and Motion' showed that nothing can go faster."

**What we assume.** "(1) The animation uses ordinary gravity, treating the light as a very fast small object, not the exact general relativity a real light beam needs. (2) The mass, distances and speed of light are relative, made-up amounts. The angles on this page are therefore not the real bending angle of real starlight. (3) There is only one mass, and it does not move. (4) The angle is measured when the light has travelled 15 units past the mass, so it is nearly, but not exactly, its final angle."

**Some new words.** "The 'Words and formulas' box below has each term with a short meaning."

**Want to know more?** "Plain pages and the original sources are listed after the tutor, because each of them says whether and how light bends, which is what you are about to guess."

*(Removed from the introduction: the paragraph "Real light bending" with the 1919 eclipse and gravitational lensing. It states the answer to guess 3. It moves to the tutor's explanation. Removed from "Your job": "see how the amount of bending changes", a hint for guess 2.)*

**Explanation (opens "Your guesses, marked."):** each guess is marked here against what the pictures showed, with no earlier verdict.
1. **Your guesses, marked.** "For the first question you guessed [yes/no]. The light was bent: the amber path turns away from the dashed line (9.5° at aim 3.0). For the second you guessed [more/less/about the same]. The curve rises as the aim gets smaller, from 7.0° at aim 4.0 to 35.2° at aim 0.8. Closer bends more, until the light falls in below about 0.75. 2. **Light passing near a mass is bent from a straight line, more so the closer it passes.** Connects to Experiment 4's "as straight as possible path" idea, now applied to something with no mass at all. *(As approved; "always bends it more" is replaced by "more, until it falls in".)*
3. **This simulation uses ordinary, first-order gravity,** treating light as a very fast small object. The angle it gives is 2 × mass ÷ (speed of light squared × aim) = 2 × 1 ÷ (4 × 2) = 0.25 of a unit of angle at aim 2.0, which is 14.3°.
4. **The real universe bends light exactly twice as much,** because real gravity (general relativity) does not just pull light off a straight path, it also curves space itself, and the light's path has to follow that curved space too. *(As approved; the full mechanism is Experiment 4's and later experiments' job.)* "At aim 2.0 that is 28.6°."
5. **Real numbers for the Sun.** "For light grazing the Sun's edge, the ordinary value is about 0.87 arcseconds and the real value about 1.75 arcseconds. An arcsecond is 1/3600 of a degree, so 1.75 arcseconds is 1.75 ÷ 3600, about 0.0005 of a degree, half of a thousandth [Physics Today, Kennefick, 2009; recheck]. The angles on this page, tens of degrees, are on a made-up scale and are far bigger than the real ones."
6. **The 1919 eclipse.** "In 1919 astronomers photographed stars near the Sun during a total solar eclipse and measured how far their light was bent. The Royal Museums Greenwich say the angle was 'a little under 2 arcseconds' [recheck], which is close to the real value and about twice the ordinary one. The Science Museum notes that the photographs from the smaller telescope at Sobral were the decisive ones [recheck]. The measurement was tiny and difficult, and this page quotes no figure for how sure it was."
7. **Gravitational lensing.** "The same bending is used by astronomers. ESA/Hubble: 'Since the amount of lensing depends on the total mass of the cluster, gravitational lensing can be used to weigh clusters.' ## 7. "Words and formulas" (collapsed, above Part 1; held-back entries marked)
- **Aim distance.** The distance from the mass to the dashed line, how close the light would pass with no gravity. Aim 2 = two units.
- **Bend (deflection).** The angle between the dashed line and the light's last direction.
- **Ordinary gravity.** The pull between masses you met in Experiments 1 to 7.
- **Relative units.** Made-up amounts; only comparisons matter.
- **Speed of light.** The fastest speed anything can have; 2 on this page.
- **Event horizon (from Experiment 7).** The distance at which even light cannot escape; here 0.5.
- **Formula (ordinary gravity), shown after the first run:** bend (in radians, the angle measure used by the formula; 1 radian = 57.3°) = 2 × mass ÷ (speed of light squared × aim). With mass 1 and speed of light 2 at aim 2: 2 × 1 ÷ (4 × 2) = 0.25, which is 14.3°.
- **Held back until the tutor's explanation:** the real-universe formula (4 × mass ÷ (speed of light squared × aim), twice the ordinary one), *general relativity*, *gravitational lensing*, *arcsecond*. They give away guess 3.

## 10. Changes made after the draft was approved (2026-11-13), at the owner's requests during the build and the browser check

The owner approved the draft with "all defaults, approved". The points below were added or changed afterwards. Each is learner-facing wording and is the owner's to approve (§28.1); the specification's Audit Update records them.

- **Rounding corrected.** The real-universe angle at aim 2.0 is 28.648°, so one decimal place gives **28.6°**, not 28.7° as this draft first said. The page uses the module's value; the four places in this file that said 28.7° now say 28.6°.
- **"Aim" explained more simply, in four rounds.** Aim is how close the light would pass to the mass if gravity did nothing, measured from the **middle of the mass** (not from the event horizon). It is counted in **units**, "a length we chose as this page's measuring stick, like one square on graph paper", not metres, kilometres or light-years. A ball rolled past a post is the daily-life picture. "8 steps either side" and "15 units past the mass" were removed because they meant nothing to the learner (the drawing now says it "zooms in on the stretch of the path near the mass", and the angle is "measured a little after the light has passed the mass"). A **scale bar** ("1 unit") was added to the drawing. The circle is "0.5 units from the middle of the mass", and aims of 0.7 and smaller fall in because the light is pulled inward as it goes.
- **A bold notice above Run** ("Run all three passes", three progress pills, 13px heading) so a learner does not forget the later passes. Parts 2 to 4 still appear after the first run; the notice says what waits for all three (the full graph, the white marks for questions 2 and 3, and the tutor).
- **Drawing and graph layout (from the browser check).** The graph has a legend row in its header and both YOUR GUESS boxes inside the plot. In Part 1 the guess ring sits on the path 3 units past the mass, the horizon label is at the bottom left, the scale bar at the top right and the aim label to the left of the dotted line. In Part 2 the labels are in a legend at the top left and the two grey Part 1 paths have different dash styles.
