> **A daily-life picture: what you can feel, and what you can't.**
>
> **Steady motion can't be felt.** Think of a smooth train or a plane cruising at a steady speed. With your eyes closed you feel nothing. A cup of coffee sits still on the table. You couldn't say whether you are moving at 500 miles an hour or parked. That is why either twin can fairly say "I'm the one standing still": while a twin moves at a steady speed, nothing in their body can tell them otherwise.
>
> **A change of speed or direction can be felt.** Now think of a car. When it brakes hard, you are thrown forward. When it speeds up, you are pushed back into your seat. When it takes a sharp turn, you are pushed sideways. The coffee cup slides. You feel all of this with your eyes closed, and so does everyone else in the car. Any change of speed or direction is called **acceleration**, and the push you feel is what acceleration feels like.
>
> **Now the twins.** The traveling twin reaches the far point and turns around. A turnaround is a change of direction, so the traveling twin feels exactly that kind of push. The twin who stays home never feels anything like it, the whole trip. (We treat the turnaround as instant. A real one would be a strong push, and nobody would miss it.)
>
> **Why this matters.** "Who is moving?" can be answered either way, depending on whom you pick as the one standing still. "Who felt a push?" has only one answer: the traveling twin. Both twins agree on it, and so does everyone watching. That felt push is a real, physical mark that the two twins' paths are not the same. It isn't just a matter of whichever twin you personally choose to call "moving."
>
> **A familiar version.** Two friends start at the same corner. One stands there all day. The other walks to the store and back. Both can say "I was moving at some point," but only one of them turned around and came back, and everyone agrees on that.
>
> **What the push is not.** The push is not what makes a clock run slow. It is a sign that the two twins' situations differ. What that difference does to their ages is what you are about to find out.

## 3. Words and formulas (new, collapsed, above Part 1)
- **Twin paradox:** the puzzle that arises when you use "a moving clock runs slow" for both twins at once. **Elapsed time / age:** how much time a clock, or a person, has lived through since the start. **Earth twin / traveling twin:** the twin who stays home and the one who goes and comes back. **Round trip:** out to a point 2 light-seconds away and back. **Turnaround:** the moment the traveling twin reverses direction; here it is treated as instant.
- **Constant speed:** not speeding up or slowing down. **Acceleration:** any change of speed or direction; you feel it as a push, like a car braking.
- **Light-second, c:** as in section 2e. **Time dilation factor:** how much slower a moving clock ticks than the lab's, as a fraction ("The moving clock"). At 0.5c it is 0.866, so the moving clock ticks 0.866 as fast.
- **Formula 1, trip time for the Earth twin = 2 × distance ÷ speed.** Example (distance 2 light-seconds, speed 0.4c, so as not to give the 0.5c answer): 2 × 2 ÷ 0.4 = 10 s.
- **Formula 2, traveling twin's time = Earth twin's time × √(1 − speed²).** Example at 0.6c: √(1 − 0.36) = 0.8, so a 10 s trip reads 10 × 0.8 = 8 s. *(The 0.5c totals are withheld until after the run.)*

## 9a. Tutor: how a turnaround leads to an age gap (new, drafted 2026-11-11 at the owner's request; approved and built the same day, with the optional jump check left out and the pointer sentence added to the introduction)

**Why it is needed.** Step 3 of the tutor's explanation says the difference "resolves the paradox" but never says how a change of direction ends in different clock readings. The introduction says only that the push is a *sign* of a different path (it says "What the push is not"). **Where it goes:** in the tutor only, after the run, as the owner's rule requires (§12, §14: the mechanism stays with the tutor). **Not in the introduction** [recommendation, owner's call]: even the odometer example hints that clocks on different paths can read differently, which would answer the guess. **The new explanation replaces old step 3 and old step 4's first sentence; the rest is kept.** New order: 1 (the traveling twin really is younger, kept), 2 (not the same kind of situation, and the two friends, kept), **3, 4 and 5 (new)**, 6 (old step 3, shortened), 7 (old step 4, kept), 8 (your guess, marked), 9 (try it in Part 2). > **3. A clock counts its own path.** Think of two drivers going from city A to city B. One takes the straight road, 6 km. The other takes a detour through city C, 10 km. Their odometers end up different, and nobody asks "how did the turn at C change the odometer?" The turn matters only because it made the two routes different. An odometer simply counts the length of the route its car took. A clock does something like it: it counts the time along the path it took, through space and through time. Two clocks that start together and meet again on different paths can read different totals. (One twist: for clocks, the detour gives the *smaller* reading, not the larger one.)
>
> **4. Why the Earth twin's view is the simple one.** The Earth twin never turns, so their point of view stays steady for the whole trip. In a steady point of view you can use the rule from "The moving clock" directly: a moving clock ticks √(1 − v²) as fast as the Earth twin's clock. At {0.5}c, the trip out takes {2} ÷ {0.5} = {4} s and the trip back takes {4} s, so the Earth twin's clock reads {8.000} s. The traveling twin's clock ticks {0.866} as fast the whole time, so it reads {8.000} × {0.866} = {6.928} s. Look at the arithmetic: **there is no acceleration in it.** The turnaround could be instant or slow, and the answer would be the same, as long as the speed is the same. That is why this page treats it as instant. So the push does not change the traveling twin's clock; it is a sign that the traveling twin took a different path.
>
> **5. Why the traveling twin can't just say "I'm the one standing still."** While cruising, they can, and from their point of view the Earth twin's clock runs slow. That argument is fine for one leg. But to get home they must turn around, and that changes their point of view. As in "At the same time... for whom?", what counts as "the same moment" depends on who is moving. A traveler heading out and a traveler heading back do not agree on what is happening on Earth "right now". The push you feel at the turnaround is the sign that this switch happened. The "the Earth twin's clock runs slow too" argument only works if you stay in one steady point of view for the whole trip, and the traveling twin cannot.

*Optional addition to step 5* **[your call; goes beyond the specification's exclusion list]:** a worked check of the switch. At {0.5}c the traveling twin's clock reads {3.464} s on each leg. In their own point of view the Earth twin's clock ticks {0.866} as fast, so it advances only {3.464} × {0.866} = {3.000} s per leg, {6.000} s in all. But the Earth twin's clock really reads {8.000} s. The missing {2.000} s is exactly the jump in "what is happening on Earth now" at the turnaround. This is the "radar time" bookkeeping that the specification lists as not introduced, and it needs a new tested module function (`turnaroundJump`) and an amendment to the specification. **Default: leave it out.**

> **6. That is what resolves the paradox.** (old step 3, shortened) The twins are not in symmetric situations: the Earth twin keeps one steady point of view the whole time, and the traveling twin does not. So there is no real contradiction in them agreeing, when they meet, that the traveling twin's clock counted less.
