## 2. Introduction (replaces everything before "What we assume")

**2a. "The question."** "Does a moving thing change length? We will look at one thing, a clock made of light, and ask: **as seen from the lab, how long is it when it is moving along its own length, compared with when it stands still?**"

**2b. "A daily-life picture."** Kept as approved: "Imagine measuring the length of a car speeding past you, versus measuring the same car parked in your driveway. In everyday life we assume you would get the same number either way: length feels like a fixed property of the car, nothing that depends on whether it is moving. This experiment checks whether that everyday assumption survives once "speeding past" means a meaningful fraction of the speed of light."

**2c. "Step 1: our ruler, a light clock."** "We need something with a length that we can follow. Our object is a **light clock**: two mirrors, one behind the other in a line, like the two ends of a train carriage, with a pulse of light bouncing between them. **The length of the clock is the distance from the back mirror to the front mirror.** That is the number this whole page is about. One trip of the pulse from the back mirror to the front mirror and back is one **tick**. We will use ticks later, only as a tool."

**2d. "Step 2: the length of the clock standing still."** "Light travels about 300,000 km in one second. The distance light travels in one second has a name: a **light-second**. We make the clock **half a light-second long**, about 150,000 km. So light needs half a second to go from one mirror to the other, and one second for a whole tick. Real light clocks are much smaller: with the mirrors only 15 cm apart, a clock would tick about a billion times a second. We use a giant clock so that the numbers are easy to read. **This standing-still length, 0.500 light-seconds, is the length we compare the moving clock with.**"

**2e. "Step 3: how the travel of light measures a length."** *(new, added 2026-11-09 at the owner's request: the page talked about how light travels without saying what that has to do with length.)* "Why do we watch a pulse of light to learn the length of a clock? Because **if you know how fast something travels and how long it takes, you know how far it went.** Distance = speed × time. Here are three everyday cases.
- **An echo.** You shout towards a cliff and hear the echo 2 seconds later. Sound travels about 340 metres every second, and the sound went there and back, so it travelled 2 × 340 = 680 metres in all. The cliff is half of that, **340 metres** away. You measured a distance with a stopwatch.
- **A walk.** "The shop is ten minutes from here" is a length told in time, because we know how fast we walk.
- **A corridor.** You walk along a corridor at a steady 1 metre every second, and it takes you 12 seconds to get from one end to the other. The corridor is 1 × 12 = **12 metres** long. This is the closest picture to our clock: the time to cross, at a known steady speed, gives the length between the two ends. (We chose a steady speed because light's speed in the lab is steady.)

*Step 3 reworded 2026-11-10 (owner-approved):* the two-lengths example now opens "Now the moving clock" with the key point (every length gives its own round-trip time, no other length gives it), defines "forward" (back mirror to front mirror) and "back", shows each line as "forward + back = whole round trip", adds a paragraph "Why is forward longer than the half second it would take if the clock stood still?" (0.5 ÷ 0.5 = 1.00 s: the pulse gains on the running-away front mirror at only c − 0.5c), and ends with "What to notice".

Light can do the same job, and it is a very good tape measure, because **in the lab it always travels at the same speed, c, in both directions** (the rule you met in "Why can't light go faster?"). So the time light takes to cross the clock tells us how long the clock is. Our clock stands still and the pulse takes **0.5 second** to go from the back mirror to the front mirror. Light goes 1 light-second every second, so it went 1 × 0.5 = **0.5 light-seconds**. That is why we call the clock half a light-second long. **A light-second is simply a length measured by light's travel.**

Now the moving clock. We will use the same idea: the pulse's travel times tell us the length. There is one extra thing to allow for, that the mirrors are moving while the pulse is in flight (Step 4). That is why the two trips take different times, and why we have to follow them carefully. But the idea is the same: **for a given speed, each possible length of the clock gives its own time for the pulse's round trip, and only that length does.** So if we know how long the round trip took, we can say how long the clock was, like reading a length off a stopwatch."

*Then, a small table, shown right after (it names no answer): "Two possible lengths for a clock moving at 0.5c, and the round trip each one would give: length 0.500 light-second: 1 s forward, 0.33 s back, 1.33 s in all. Length 0.400 light-second: 0.8 s forward, 0.27 s back, 1.07 s in all. A different length, a different round trip."* (Numbers from the module when built: 0.4 ÷ 0.5 = 0.8; 0.4 ÷ 1.5 = 0.267; 1.067.)

**2f. "Step 4: what moves, and why length matters for the pulse."** "Now imagine the same clock moving along its own length, front mirror first. Both mirrors move together at the same speed, so the length of the clock does not change while it moves. Neither mirror gains on the other." *Then, as approved on 2026-11-09:* "The important point is that **the pulse is not carried along by the clock**. Once it leaves the back mirror, nothing pushes it forward with the clock. In the lab it keeps travelling at the speed of light, **c**, and the clock's motion adds nothing to that speed. The mirrors move, but the pulse's speed stays c. So the distance that matters is the one **between the pulse and the mirror it is heading for**, and that distance changes." Bullets (Forward trip with the bus; Return trip with the friend) and the walkway ball, as approved.

*Then (new), to tie it to length:* "Notice what this means: **the longer the clock, the longer each trip of the pulse, so the longer the tick.** The length of the clock and the time of one tick are tied together. That tie is our tool."

*Worked example, reframed as trying one possible length:* "Let us try **one possible length** for the moving clock at half the speed of light (0.5c): the same length as when it stands still, half a light-second. Going forward, the pulse closes the gap at c − 0.5c = 0.5c, so it takes 0.5 ÷ 0.5 = **1 second**. In that time it travels 1 light-second: the 0.5 light-second length plus 0.5 light-second more, because the front mirror moved that far. Coming back, it closes the gap at c + 0.5c = 1.5c, so it takes 0.5 ÷ 1.5 = about **0.33 second**. One tick would take about 1.33 seconds. **This is only what that one possible length gives.** We do not yet know whether it is the real length. Finding the real one is the job of this page."

**2g. "Step 5: the rule we keep."** (As approved; unchanged in meaning.) "In "A moving clock" and "Why time dilation happens" you saw that a moving clock ticks more slowly, as measured by the lab's clocks. That effect is called **time dilation**. We assume that it works for every moving clock, however it is turned. So one tick of the moving clock must take the time that time dilation gives. We also keep the rule from "Why time dilation happens" and "Why can't light go faster?": in the lab, light always goes at the same speed, **c**, in both directions."

**2h. "Step 6: how we find the length."** *(new, replaces the old Step 5)* "We cannot lay a ruler along a clock that is speeding past, and measuring a moving length directly needs an idea from a later chapter. So here we find the length with light, as in Step 3. We know how long one tick of the moving clock must take (Step 5), and we know that each length of the clock gives its own tick (Steps 3 and 4). So **we ask: what length must the moving clock have, as seen from the lab, for its tick to take exactly that long?** That length is the answer to the question of this page. We will compare it with the standing-still length, half a light-second."

**2h-2. "A trap to avoid: the clocks are not built shorter."** *(new, owner-approved 2026-11-10, after the owner asked why a closer pair of mirrors is not simply obvious)* Sits after Step 6 and before "What you will see". Says that a clock with its mirrors closer together does tick faster (shorter clock, shorter tick; the tie from Steps 3 and 4), but that nobody moves the mirrors to make a clock tick faster. The tick is what we already know (Step 5) and the length is what we do not know, so the tie is used backwards, like the echo that tells how far the cliff is. The clocks drawn are **trial lengths**, drawn side by side to compare; which trial matches the known tick is what the learner guesses. No numbers 0.433 or 1.155 and no verdict.

**2h-3. "A small worked example with invented numbers."** *(owner-approved 2026-11-10)* Suppose a tick of exactly 2.0 s at 0.5c (stated to be made up, not the real tick).
