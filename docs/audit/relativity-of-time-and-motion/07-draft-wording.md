## 2. Introduction (replaces everything before "What we assume")

**2a. "The question."** (kept) "If two things happen 'at the same time,' is that a fact everyone agrees on, no matter how they are moving?"

**2b. "A daily-life picture."** (kept as approved) "Imagine two lightning bolts strike, one at each end of a train platform, at exactly the same instant as measured by someone standing on the platform. Would a passenger riding past on a fast train, watching the same two flashes, necessarily agree they happened at the same instant too? Ordinary experience never puts this question to a real test, because nothing we deal with day to day moves anywhere near fast enough for the answer to matter — but this experiment does."

**2c. "Step 1: a block and a flash."** *(new, replaces "What happens"; the two sentences that gave the answers away are removed)* "We use a **block** like the light clock of "Does motion change length?": a straight bar that moves along its own length. Standing still it is **half a light-second** long. A **light-second** is the distance light travels in one second, about 300,000 km, so half a light-second is about 150,000 km. (A real block would be far shorter. We use a giant one so that the numbers are easy to read.) At one moment, a flash of light is released from the **exact middle** of the block. It spreads out from there, one part towards the back end and one part towards the front end, at the speed of light, **c**."

**2d. "Step 2: two events."** *(new)* "Two things now happen, and we will watch both. **The flash reaches the back end.** **The flash reaches the front end.** Each is an **event**: something that happens at one place at one moment. If the block is standing still, the flash has the same distance to go each way, so it reaches both ends at the same time. Now the block moves along its own length at **0.5c**, half the speed of light, with its front end leading. Light still travels at c in the lab, in both directions."

**2e. "Step 3: who is asking, and with which clocks."** *(new; the approved "point of view" paragraph, kept in meaning and shortened)* "To say **when** the flash reached an end, we need clocks, and it matters whose clocks. **The lab's point of view** means what someone standing still in the lab, watching the block go by, would measure with the lab's clocks. The lab has many clocks standing still in a row, all set to agree with each other, as in "Two clocks at rest". A reading on one of them is called **lab time**. **The block's own point of view** means what someone riding along with the block, at the same speed right beside it, would measure with the clocks that travel with the block, one at each end, set to agree with each other. A reading on those is called the **block's own time**. Both points of view are equally real. The experiment asks how they compare."

**2f. "What you will see."** *(new)* "Two pictures, one above the other, run together. The top picture is **the block's own point of view**: the block does not move in it. ## 3. Words and formulas (new box, collapsed, above Part 1)

- **Block:** a straight bar, half a light-second long when it stands still. **Rest length:** its length when it stands still. **Length seen from the lab:** its length as measured by the lab, when it moves along its own length (shorter than the rest length, as found in "Does motion change length?"). **Back end / front end:** the end the block moves away from, and the end it moves towards.
- **Flash:** a short burst of light released from the exact middle of the block. **Light-second:** the distance light travels in one second, about 300,000 km. **c:** the speed of light, 300,000 km every second; "0.5c" is half of that.
- **Event:** something that happens at one place and one moment (here, the flash reaching an end). **Simultaneity:** two events happening at the same time. **Relativity of simultaneity:** the idea that two events can be simultaneous for one observer and not for another.
- **Lab, lab clocks, lab time:** the place where the experiment is set up, its many clocks standing still and set to agree, and a reading on them. **Block's own clocks, block's own time:** the clocks that travel with the block, one at each end, set to agree, and a reading on them. **Point of view / reference frame:** what someone measures with their own clocks and rulers while at rest in their own frame. **At rest / moving / along its own length:** as in "Two clocks at rest" and "Does motion change length?".
- **Formula, time = distance ÷ speed.** Example from the page's numbers: light goes at c, which is 1 light-second every second, so to cover 0.25 light-second it needs 0.25 ÷ 1 = 0.25 second. *(Withheld until after the run, because it gives the first guess away: the lab's arrival-time sums for the moving block. The formula above uses no moving block.)*

**Caption 1, "How to read the two pictures"** (from the start; no times, no answers):
- **Two pictures.** The top picture is the block's own point of view, the bottom picture is the lab's point of view. Both show **the same flash**, released from the same place at the same moment, and the same two events. They run at the same time.
- **The block** is the pair of thick lines joined by the axis: a **blue line** is the back end and a **purple line** is the front end. In the top picture they do not move. In the bottom picture they move to the right at the speed written in the title. The **dashed line** marks the place where the flash was released. It stays where it is in each picture.
- **The flash** is the two dots that start on the dashed line and move apart, one towards each end, at the speed of light. A dot is **amber** while it is on its way and turns **green** when it has reached its end. That moment is an event.
- **The readouts** beside each picture give the time at which each end was reached, in that picture's own clocks, once the dot has turned green. "—" means not reached yet.
- **Playback time** runs the pictures. It is not a reading on any clock in the experiment.
- **Light-seconds and c:** a light-second is the distance light goes in one second (about 300,000 km). 0.5c is half the speed of light.
- **When the run ends:** the run stops when the last dot has turned green.

**Caption 2, "How to read the event-time strip"** (with the strip, after the run):
- **The strip** is a time scale in seconds: further right means later. Time 0 is the moment the flash was released, the same event in both frames.
- **The top row is the lab's clocks** and **the bottom row is the block's own clocks.** Each dot is an event: the blue dot is the flash reaching the back end, the purple dot is the flash reaching the front end. Where a dot sits is the time its clocks gave.
- **The double arrow** between the two dots on the lab row is the **gap**: how much later one end was reached than the other, on the lab's clocks. A pair of dots on top of each other means the gap is 0.
- **The white markers** are your guesses. They sit on the strip but do not move a dot. Each is only a marker.

*A worked moment, step by step (at 0.5c).* (1) The block is 0.500 light-seconds long at rest. The flash starts in the middle, so each end is 0.25 light-second away when the flash is released, as the block's own clocks see it. (2) **The block's own clocks:** the flash goes 0.25 light-second at c, so each end is reached at 0.25 ÷ 1 = **0.250 s**. (3) **The lab's clocks:** as seen from the lab the block is shorter, 0.433 light-seconds, so each end is 0.2165 light-second from the middle at the moment of the flash. But the ends move. The flash and the **back end** come towards each other, so the distance between them closes at c + 0.5c = 1.5c: 0.2165 ÷ 1.5 = **0.144 s**. The flash chases the **front end**, which runs away, so the distance closes at c − 0.5c = 0.5c: 0.2165 ÷ 0.5 = **0.433 s**. (4) The lab's gap is 0.433 − 0.144 = **0.289 s**. The block's own gap is 0.

*What to look for* (each: **Look at** / **What it means** / **Why it matters**; an item appears only once its feature is drawn):
1. **Look at** the two dots on the lab row. **What it means:** the lab's clocks say the flash reached the back end at 0.144 s and the front end at 0.433 s. **Why it matters:** these are the lab's times for the two events, the answer to your first guess.
2. **Look at** the double arrow on the lab row. **What it means:** the two arrivals are 0.289 s apart on the lab's clocks. **Why it matters:** it turns "at the same time or not" into a number you can see: 0 means together.
3. **Look at** the pair of dots on the block's own row. **What it means:** the block's own clocks say both ends were reached at 0.250 s, so the gap is 0. **Why it matters:** this is the block's answer, for you to compare with the lab's.
4. **Look at** the two rows together, and at the same blue and purple dots in each. **What it means:** it is the same flash and the same two events. The lab row puts the dots 0.289 s apart; the block row puts them together. **Why it matters:** it is exactly what your second guess asked about. The tutor will help you work out what to make of it.
5. **Look at** the white markers and the dots they point to. **What it means:** each marker shows the choice you made. They only show your guesses. **Why it matters:** they put what you expected next to what was found.
